Order of operations and exponents

MATH 015 textbook section: 3.1

An exponent says how many times to multiply a number by itself: 5² = 5 × 5 = 25, and 3³ = 3 × 3 × 3 = 27. Work in this order: parentheses, exponents, multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction.

In astronomy: Kepler’s third law, p² = a³. For a planet 4 AU from the Sun, a³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64.

TI-30XS: square a number with x²; use ^ for any other power (for example, 9.54 ^ 3).

Fractions, decimals, and percent

MATH 015 textbook section: 4.1–6.3

A percent is a fraction out of 100: 25% = 25/100 = 0.25. Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old, written as a percent.

In astronomy: the Big Bang made about 25% helium by mass: 4 of every 16 particles. Carbon dioxide in Earth’s air rose from 280 to 420 parts per million, and (420 − 280) ÷ 280 = 0.5, a 50% increase.

TI-30XS: enter fractions with n/d; switch between fraction and decimal with ◄►.

Ratios, rates, and proportions

MATH 015 textbook section: 7.1–7.2

A ratio compares two quantities by division. "How many times as big" is a ratio: Jupiter’s diameter ÷ Earth’s diameter = 139,820 ÷ 12,742 ≈ 11, so Jupiter is about 11 times as wide. A rate is a ratio with units, like 70 km/s per megaparsec. A proportion sets two ratios equal; solve it with cross products.

In astronomy: scale models, light-travel times, and rules like "double the distance and gravity becomes one-fourth as strong."

Tip: when a problem asks "how many times," divide the larger quantity by the smaller one.

Unit conversions

MATH 015 textbook section: 7.3

Multiply by a conversion factor written as a fraction equal to 1, so the units you don’t want cancel: 3 hours × (60 minutes ÷ 1 hour) = 180 minutes.

In astronomy: seconds to years (1 year ≈ 31,600,000 seconds), kilometers to AU (1 AU = 150 million km), and light-years to kilometers (1 light-year ≈ 9.46 trillion km).

Tip: write the units on every number and cross out the ones that cancel.

Squares and square roots

MATH 015 textbook section: 5.6

A square root undoes squaring: √49 = 7 because 7² = 49. Most square roots are decimals, so use the calculator.

In astronomy: the last step of Kepler’s third law (p = √(a³)), the inverse-square law for brightness (3 times farther means 3² = 9 times dimmer, so 9 times dimmer means √9 = 3 times farther), and the Lorentz factor in relativity.

TI-30XS: press 2nd then x² for √, type the number, and close the parenthesis.

Formulas and simple equations

MATH 015 textbook section: 3.3–3.5

To evaluate a formula, substitute the values and follow the order of operations. To find an unknown in a simple formula, undo the operations in reverse order.

In astronomy: Hubble’s law, v = H₀ × d. If v = 7,000 km/s and H₀ = 70, then 7,000 = 70 × d, so d = 7,000 ÷ 70 = 100 megaparsecs. Parallax: d = 1 ÷ p.

Tip: write the formula first, then a second line with the numbers substituted in.

π and circles

MATH 015 textbook section: 5.3

A circle’s circumference is C = 2πr and its area is A = πr². Formulas for spheres, such as surface area 4πr² and volume (4/3)πr³, are always given when you need them; you only evaluate them.

In astronomy: orbits, the area light spreads over (4πd²), and planets’ volumes and densities.

TI-30XS: use the π key rather than typing 3.14, and round only at the end.

Scientific notation (new in this course)

MATH 015 textbook section: not in MATH 015; taught in Module 1

Astronomy deals with numbers far too big or small to write out. Scientific notation writes a number as a value from 1 to 10 times a power of ten: 150,000,000 km = 1.5 × 10⁸ km, and 0.0000001 m = 1 × 10⁻⁷ m. A positive power of ten moves the decimal point right; a negative one moves it left.

To multiply, multiply the front numbers and add the powers: (3 × 10⁸) × (2 × 10³) = 6 × 10¹¹. To divide, divide the front numbers and subtract the powers. Once you enter the numbers correctly, the calculator does this for you.

TI-30XS: enter 3 × 10⁸ as 3 ×10ⁿ 8. For a negative power, use the (−) key, not the subtraction key. To see answers in scientific notation, press mode and choose SCI. Answer boxes on this site also accept e-notation: type 3e8 for 3 × 10⁸.

Reading graphs

MATH 015 textbook section: 7.5

Each point on a graph has an x-value (horizontal axis) and a y-value (vertical axis). Always read the axis labels and units first.

In astronomy: the simulations graph brightness over time (light curves), speed against distance (Hubble’s law), and luminosity against temperature (the H-R diagram). Some axes multiply by 10 at each tick, so equal steps mean "10 times more." Those axes are always labeled, and you only need to read them, not calculate with them.

Tip: on the H-R diagram, temperature increases to the left, the opposite of most graphs.

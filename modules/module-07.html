Relativity: Space, Time, and Gravity

Einstein showed that space and time are not the fixed stage we imagine. Moving clocks run slow, moving objects shrink, mass is a form of energy, and gravity is the curvature of spacetime itself. These ideas sound like science fiction, but they are tested every day, including by the GPS in your phone.

Part A

Special relativity

What happens when things move very, very fast.

7.1

What is relative, and what is absolute

In 1905, a 26-year-old patent clerk named Albert Einstein published a theory that overturned centuries of assumptions about space and time. We call it . Despite its name, its core is about what is not relative.

Motion, we already know, is relative. If you sit in a smoothly moving train, you can say you are at rest and the landscape is moving; someone on the platform says the train is moving. Neither is wrong. Each describes events from a different . Galileo (Module 2) realized that the laws of motion work the same way for everyone moving steadily.

Einstein built his theory on two statements, or postulates:

  1. The laws of nature are the same for everyone moving at constant velocity. No experiment can tell you whether you are "really" moving or "really" at rest.
  2. The speed of light in a vacuum is the same for everyone, about 300,000 km/s, no matter how fast they or the light source are moving.
Think of it this way

Throw a ball forward at 30 km/h from a train moving at 100 km/h, and someone on the platform sees it go 130 km/h. Common sense says light should work the same way. It doesn’t: shine a flashlight forward from the train, and both you and the person on the platform measure exactly the same speed of light. Everything strange about relativity follows from taking that fact seriously.

Common misconception

“Relativity says everything is relative.” Einstein’s theory rests on absolutes: the laws of physics and the speed of light are the same for everyone. What turns out to be relative are measurements of time, length, and simultaneity, which depend on how you are moving.

7.2

The constant speed of light

Scientists in the 1800s expected light to behave like sound, traveling through some medium, and that Earth’s motion through that medium would change light’s measured speed depending on direction. In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley performed an extremely precise experiment to measure this difference. They found none. Light moved at the same speed in every direction, whatever Earth’s motion.

Einstein accepted this result at face value. If light’s speed is the same for everyone, then something else must give, and that something is our ideas about time and space. It also means light cannot be caught: no matter how fast you chase a light beam, it still races away from you at 300,000 km/s. That makes the speed of light a universal speed limit for matter, energy, and information.

Think of it this way

Imagine a racer who always stays exactly 300,000 km/s ahead of you, no matter how hard you run, whether you are standing still or riding the fastest rocket ever built. You could never gain on it. For light, that is simply how the universe works.

7.3

Time dilation

Einstein’s favorite way to see the consequences was a thought experiment with a : a pulse of light bouncing between two mirrors, each round trip one "tick."

Now put an identical clock on a fast spaceship flying past you. Inside the ship, the light bounces straight up and down as usual. But from your point of view, the ship moves sideways while the light travels, so the light follows a longer, zig-zag diagonal path. Since light moves at the same speed for you, the longer path takes longer. You see the moving clock tick more slowly. This is .

The amount is given by the :

γ = 1 ÷ √(1 − v²/c²)

A moving clock runs slow by a factor of γ. At everyday speeds γ is essentially 1; at 87% of the speed of light it is 2; at 99%, about 7. And the effect is symmetric: the space travelers see your clock running slow, since from their point of view you are the one moving.

Think of it this way

Think of the zig-zag path in the moving clock like a ball player running the bases: someone watching from the stands sees a longer path than the player feels themselves running. For light, which always goes the same speed, a longer path means a longer time.

7.4

Length contraction and simultaneity

If time stretches for moving objects, lengths must shrink so that everyone agrees on what happens. means a moving object is measured to be shorter along its direction of motion by the same factor γ. A spaceship moving at 87% of light speed looks half as long as it does at rest. Its width and height are unchanged.

Even the idea of "at the same time" becomes relative. Two events that are simultaneous for one observer may happen at different times for another observer moving relative to the first. This is what resolves the apparent contradictions, such as each twin seeing the other’s clock run slow.

Think of it this way

Space and time are like the length and width of a rectangle viewed at different angles. Turn it, and its shadow gets longer in one direction and shorter in another, but the rectangle itself hasn’t changed. Moving at different speeds is like viewing a single, unchanging reality called {term("spacetime","spacetime")} from different angles: time and space trade off, while the underlying spacetime stays the same.

7.5

E = mc² and the cosmic speed limit

Relativity also revealed that mass and energy are two forms of the same thing, related by the most famous equation in physics, E = mc². Because c² is enormous (9 × 10¹⁶ m²/s²), a tiny amount of mass holds a vast amount of energy. This is the energy source of the Sun and all stars (Module 9), nuclear power plants, and nuclear weapons.

The same idea explains the speed limit. As you push an object faster, more and more of the energy you add goes into its momentum and energy rather than into speed. Approaching the speed of light would take infinite energy, so no material object can ever get there. Particle accelerators like CERN’s Large Hadron Collider push protons to 99.999999% of the speed of light, and their behavior matches Einstein’s equations exactly. Only massless things, like light itself, travel at the speed of light, and they always do.

Think of it this way

Pushing an object toward the speed of light is like pushing a cart up a hill that gets steeper and steeper, becoming a vertical wall right at the speed of light. Each push gets you a little closer, but you can never reach the top.

7.6

The twin paradox and time travel

Suppose one twin stays on Earth while the other flies to a distant star at high speed and returns. The Earth twin sees the traveler’s clock run slow, so the traveler should come home younger. But the traveler could say that Earth was the one moving, so the Earth twin should be younger. Which is it?

The situation isn’t symmetric. To come home, the traveler must fire her engines, slow down, turn around, and speed up again. That acceleration, and the change of reference frame that comes with it, breaks the symmetry. In spacetime, the traveler’s path is bent while the Earth twin’s is straight, and the bent path has less time along it. The traveling twin really does return younger. This is not a paradox, just a surprise.

Part B

General relativity

Gravity as the shape of spacetime.

7.7

The equivalence principle

Special relativity handles steady motion. Einstein spent the next ten years extending it to acceleration and gravity, and in 1915 he published . It began with what he called "the happiest thought of my life."

Imagine you are in a closed elevator with no windows. If it sits on Earth, you feel your normal weight. If instead it is in deep space, accelerating upward at 9.8 m/s², you feel exactly the same. And if the elevator cable snaps and you fall freely, you float, just as you would far from any planet. Einstein’s says that no experiment inside the box can tell the difference: the effects of gravity and of acceleration are exactly equivalent.

Think of it this way

When a plane accelerates down the runway, you are pressed back into your seat as if a sideways gravity had switched on. Einstein took this seriously: there is no way to tell, from the inside, whether that push is due to acceleration or to gravity.

Two predictions follow. First, gravity bends light: in an accelerating elevator, a beam of light entering one side would appear to curve toward the floor, so it must do the same in gravity. Second, time runs slower in stronger gravity: a clock at the bottom of a building ticks slightly slower than one at the top. This has been measured with atomic clocks separated by just 30 centimeters in height.

7.8

Gravity as curved spacetime

Einstein’s revolutionary conclusion was that gravity is not a force pulling objects across space. Instead, mass and energy curve the four-dimensional fabric of , and objects in free fall simply follow the straightest possible paths through that curved spacetime. The physicist John Wheeler summed it up: spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve.

Think of it this way

A common picture is a stretched rubber sheet with a bowling ball in the middle. The ball makes a dip, and a marble rolled across the sheet curves around it, or circles it like a planet. It is a helpful image with two cautions: the real curvature is of spacetime, not just space, and it works without any "gravity pulling down" on the sheet. Much of what we feel as gravity actually comes from the curvature of time.

Why don’t we notice curved spacetime? Near Earth, the curvature is tiny, so Newton’s law of gravity (Module 3) remains an excellent approximation. General relativity only makes a noticeable difference where gravity is very strong or measurements are very precise.

7.9

Testing general relativity

General relativity has passed every test scientists have devised:

  • Mercury’s orbit. Mercury’s elliptical orbit slowly rotates, or precesses. Newton’s gravity couldn’t account for 43 arcseconds per century of that shift. General relativity explains it exactly, which was Einstein’s first success.
  • Bending starlight. During the total solar eclipse of 1919, Arthur Eddington’s expedition measured stars near the Sun shifted from their usual positions by about the amount Einstein predicted. The result made Einstein world-famous.
  • . Galaxies and clusters of galaxies bend the light of more distant objects behind them, creating arcs, multiple images, and rings. Astronomers use the amount of bending to measure the lenses’ masses, including dark matter (Module 13).
  • Clocks in gravity. Gravitational time dilation has been measured with rockets, airplanes, and laboratory clocks, and it must be corrected for in GPS.

Relativity in your pocket

GPS satellites orbit at about 20,000 km altitude and 14,000 km/h. Because of their speed, special relativity makes their clocks run slow by about 7 microseconds per day. Because gravity is weaker up there, general relativity makes them run fast by about 45 microseconds per day. The net effect is 38 microseconds per day fast. That sounds tiny, but GPS works by timing signals that travel at the speed of light: 38 microseconds of error is more than 11 km of position error, every day. The system corrects for both effects.

7.10

Black holes

If enough mass is squeezed into a small enough space, spacetime curves so much that nothing, not even light, can escape. That is a . Its boundary, the point of no return, is the , and its radius, the , is about 3 km for every solar mass:

R = 2GM ÷ c² ≈ 3 km × (mass in solar masses)
Think of it this way

Picture a river flowing toward a waterfall. Far upstream, a strong swimmer can swim against the current. Closer in, the current speeds up, until at one point it flows faster than anyone could swim. That point is like an event horizon: beyond it, even light, the fastest swimmer of all, is carried inward.

Near a black hole, relativity’s effects become extreme. A distant observer would see a clock falling toward the horizon tick slower and slower, its light growing redder and dimmer, never quite seeming to cross. For someone falling in, nothing special happens at the horizon itself, but tidal forces stretch objects approaching a small black hole in a process nicknamed "spaghettification."

Common misconception

“Black holes suck in everything around them.” Far from a black hole, its gravity is the same as any object of equal mass. If the Sun were replaced by a black hole of the same mass, the planets would keep orbiting exactly as before (though it would get very cold and dark).

7.11

Gravitational waves

General relativity predicts that accelerating masses send out ripples in spacetime, , that travel at the speed of light, alternately stretching and squeezing space as they pass. Einstein himself doubted they could ever be detected; the effect is incredibly small.

Think of it this way

Drop a stone in a still pond and ripples spread out from it. Two massive objects whirling around each other are like a pair of stones stirring the water nonstop, sending out steady ripples that grow stronger as they spiral together, until one final splash as they merge.

On September 14, 2015, the twin LIGO detectors in Washington and Louisiana recorded the passing of gravitational waves from two black holes, about 36 and 29 times the Sun’s mass, merging 1.3 billion light-years away. The waves stretched each 4-km detector arm by less than one-thousandth the width of a proton. The detection earned the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics. Since then, LIGO and its partner detectors have recorded hundreds of mergers, including colliding neutron stars in 2017 whose explosion was also seen by telescopes across the electromagnetic spectrum (Module 11).

7.12

Relativity today, and its limits

Relativity is not an exotic theory reserved for black holes. It underlies GPS navigation, the design of particle accelerators, the medical PET scanners that detect matter–antimatter annihilation, and our understanding of how the Sun shines. Every astronomical calculation involving high speeds, strong gravity, or precise timing uses it.

Yet general relativity is incomplete. At the center of a black hole and at the very beginning of the universe, its equations predict infinite density, a sign that the theory breaks down. There, gravity must be combined with quantum mechanics, the physics of the very small that you will meet in Module 8. Finding that combined theory, often called quantum gravity, is one of the great unsolved problems in physics.

Think of it this way

Newton’s gravity wasn’t wrong so much as incomplete: it is a special case of Einstein’s, and still works perfectly for launching spacecraft. Physicists expect general relativity to be a special case of some deeper theory in the same way.

Pause and apply: why can’t we just travel faster?

Why can’t a spacecraft reach the nearest star in less than 4.24 years as measured on Earth? (Nothing can outrun light, which takes 4.24 years. But at speeds close to light’s, time dilation means the crew could make the trip in much less than 4.24 years of their own time.)

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • Special relativity rests on two absolutes: the laws of physics and the speed of light are the same for everyone moving at constant velocity.
  • Moving clocks run slow by the Lorentz factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²); moving objects contract along their direction of motion; simultaneity is relative.
  • Space and time combine into spacetime. Muons reaching the ground and atomic clocks on aircraft confirm time dilation.
  • E = mc²: mass is a form of energy. No material object can reach the speed of light, because it would take infinite energy.
  • The traveling twin ages less because only she changes frame; her bent path through spacetime has less elapsed time.
  • The equivalence principle says gravity and acceleration are indistinguishable, so gravity bends light and slows time.
  • In general relativity, mass curves spacetime and free-falling objects follow the straightest paths through it.
  • Mercury’s orbit, starlight bending in 1919, gravitational lensing, and GPS corrections all confirm general relativity.
  • A black hole’s event horizon has a radius of about 3 km per solar mass; near it, time slows dramatically. The Event Horizon Telescope has imaged two.
  • Gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime, were first detected by LIGO in 2015 from merging black holes.

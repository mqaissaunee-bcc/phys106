Part A

From watching to understanding

Two thousand years of careful observation, clever models, and hard-won evidence.

2.1

Why ancient people watched the sky

Long before telescopes, people all over the world watched the sky closely, and for practical reasons. The sky was a clock, a calendar, and a compass. Farmers needed to know when to plant; herders when to move; travelers which way to go. The patterns overhead repeat reliably, so anyone who learned them could predict the seasons.

Think of it this way

Your phone tells you the time, the date, and where you are. For most of human history, the sky did all three jobs. Losing track of it would have been like losing your phone, your calendar, and your map at once.

A few examples show how widespread and sophisticated this watching was:

  • Egypt. The yearly flood of the Nile, which made farming possible, arrived around the time the bright star Sirius first reappeared in the dawn sky after months hidden in the Sun's glare. Its return was a signal to prepare.
  • Britain. Stonehenge, built in stages beginning about 5,000 years ago, is aligned with the sunrise at the June solstice and the sunset at the December solstice.
  • Mesoamerica. Maya astronomers tracked the cycles of Venus so precisely that their tables, preserved in a book known as the Dresden Codex, predicted its appearances for centuries.
  • China. Court astronomers kept detailed records for thousands of years, including a "guest star" in 1054 that we now know was a supernova. Its remains are the Crab Nebula.
  • The Pacific. Polynesian navigators crossed thousands of kilometers of open ocean by memorizing where key stars rise and set along the horizon.
  • The Islamic world. From about the 800s, scholars in Baghdad and elsewhere translated, preserved, and improved Greek astronomy. Many bright stars still carry Arabic names, such as Aldebaran, Betelgeuse, and Rigel.

The sky is also built into ordinary language. The month comes from the Moon's cycle. The seven days of the week match the seven objects that ancient observers saw moving against the stars: the Sun, the Moon, and five planets. You can hear them in Sunday, Monday, and Saturday, and in Spanish martes (Mars), miércoles (Mercury), jueves (Jupiter), and viernes (Venus).

2.2

A round Earth, measured

Many cultures observed the sky carefully, but the ancient Greeks took a distinctive further step: they tried to explain what they saw with models built on natural causes and reasoning, rather than on the actions of gods. That habit is the root of modern science.

By about 350 BCE, Aristotle had collected solid evidence that Earth is a sphere. During a lunar eclipse, Earth's shadow on the Moon is always curved, and only a sphere casts a round shadow from every direction. Travelers heading south see stars appear above the southern horizon that are never visible farther north, which happens only on a curved surface.

Common misconception

"People in Columbus's time thought Earth was flat." Educated Europeans had known Earth was round for nearly 2,000 years. The argument over Columbus's voyage was about Earth's size, and his critics, who said it was too far to sail west to Asia, were right.

Eratosthenes measures the Earth

Around 240 BCE, Eratosthenes, the head of the great library at Alexandria in Egypt, measured Earth's size with nothing more than shadows and geometry. He learned that at noon on the summer solstice in the town of Syene (modern Aswan), far to the south, the Sun shone straight down a well: it was directly overhead. At the same moment in Alexandria, a vertical stick cast a shadow showing the Sun was about 7.2° from overhead.

Because the Sun is so far away, its rays arrive essentially parallel everywhere on Earth. So the 7.2° difference must be the angle between the two cities as measured from Earth's center. And 7.2° is one-fiftieth of a full circle, so Earth's circumference must be 50 times the distance between the cities.

Eratosthenes estimated the distance as 5,000 stadia, giving a circumference of 250,000 stadia. We are not sure exactly how long his stadion was, but by most estimates his answer was within about 15% of the true value of 40,075 km, and possibly much closer. It was a remarkable result for the third century BCE.

2.3

The Earth-centered universe

Most Greek thinkers placed a motionless Earth at the center of everything, with the Sun, Moon, planets, and stars moving around it: a model. This was not a foolish idea. It matched everyday experience: we do not feel Earth move, objects fall straight down, and, as you learned in Module 1, no one could see the stellar parallax that a moving Earth should produce.

The hard part was the planets, especially their occasional backward loops of apparent retrograde motion. If everything circles Earth, why would a planet ever reverse? Around 150 CE, Claudius Ptolemy of Alexandria published the most complete answer. In the , each planet rides on a small circle, an , whose center travels around Earth on a larger circle. When the planet's motion on the epicycle runs opposite to the epicycle's forward motion, the planet appears to back up.

Think of it this way

Picture the spinning-teacup ride at an amusement park. Each cup spins on its own small platform while the whole platform turns. Watch a rider from outside and you see them loop: mostly moving forward, but backward for a moment each time their cup spins them around. A planet on an epicycle moves the same way.

Ptolemy's model was complicated, but it worked. It predicted planetary positions well enough to be used for about 1,500 years. His book came down to Europe through Arabic translation, which is why we know it by an Arabic-derived name, the Almagest.

One Greek did propose the alternative. Around 280 BCE, Aristarchus argued that Earth orbits the Sun, a model. His idea did not catch on, largely because of the missing parallax: if Earth moved, the stars should shift, and they did not seem to.

2.4

Copernicus and Tycho

In 1543, the Polish astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus published On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres, placing the Sun at the center with Earth as one of the planets. This began what we now call the . His model explained retrograde motion simply, as Simulation 2.1 shows, and it let him work out the planets' relative distances from the Sun for the first time.

But his model was not more accurate than Ptolemy's. Copernicus kept the ancient belief that orbits must be perfect circles, and circles do not fit the real motions. To match observations, he still needed epicycles. With no clear advantage in prediction, and with the parallax problem unsolved, most scholars were not persuaded.

What the debate needed was better data, and the Danish nobleman Tycho Brahe supplied it. Working without a telescope, Tycho built large, precise instruments and measured planetary positions for more than 20 years, to within about 1 arcminute, roughly the width of a coin seen from 80 meters away. That was about ten times better than anyone before him. He showed that a "new star" in 1572 and a comet in 1577 were far beyond the Moon, so the heavens were not unchanging as Aristotle had taught.

Think of it this way

Tycho was like the engineer who logs every measurement carefully before anyone proposes a design. His data did not settle the question by themselves; Tycho actually favored a hybrid model in which the planets orbit the Sun while the Sun orbits Earth. But his precise log was what finally allowed someone else to find the right answer.

2.5

Kepler's laws

After Tycho died in 1601, his assistant Johannes Kepler inherited the data. Kepler spent years trying to fit Mars's motion with circles. His best circle model matched most of Tycho's observations but missed some by 8 arcminutes. Earlier astronomers would have shrugged at such a small error. Kepler trusted Tycho's measurements, rejected his own model, and tried other shapes. The answer turned out to be the ellipse, and it led him to three laws that describe every planet's orbit.

First law: orbits are ellipses

Each planet orbits the Sun in an , with the Sun at one of its two foci (not at the center). How stretched an ellipse is is measured by its , from 0 for a perfect circle toward 1 for a very elongated oval. Half of the ellipse's long axis, the , equals the planet's average distance from the Sun.

Think of it this way

Stick two pushpins into cardboard, loop a piece of string around them, and trace the curve you get by keeping a pencil pulling the string tight. That is an ellipse, and the pins are its foci. Move the pins together and the ellipse becomes a circle; spread them apart and it stretches. Try it; it takes two minutes.

Second law: equal areas in equal times

A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal amounts of time. So a planet moves fastest at , its closest point to the Sun, and slowest at , its farthest point.

Think of it this way

A skateboarder in a half-pipe moves fastest at the bottom and slowest near the top of each ramp. A planet falls toward the Sun, speeding up to its fastest at perihelion, then coasts back out, slowing all the way to aphelion.

Third law: bigger orbits take longer

The square of a planet's orbital period equals the cube of its average distance: p² = a³, when the period p is in years and the distance a is in astronomical units. Outer planets not only have farther to go; they also move more slowly.

Worked example: Kepler's third law

Jupiter's average distance from the Sun is 5.2 AU. How long is its year?

p² = a³ = 5.2³ ≈ 141 → p = √141 ≈ 11.9 years

Jupiter takes almost 12 years to circle the Sun, which is why it appears in a different zodiac constellation roughly each year.

2.6

Galileo's evidence

Kepler's ellipses made the Sun-centered model far more accurate than Ptolemy's, but critics still had three serious objections. Galileo Galilei, working in Italy, answered all of them, and from 1609 onward he did it with a new tool: the telescope.

Objection 1: "If Earth moved, we would feel it, and things would be left behind." Through experiments with rolling balls, Galileo showed that a moving object stays in motion unless something acts to stop it. Everything on a moving Earth moves along with it.

Think of it this way

Toss a ball straight up on a smooth-running train and it lands back in your hand, not at the back of the car. The ball was already moving forward with you and keeps going. Birds, clouds, and falling objects share Earth's motion the same way.

Objection 2: "The heavens are perfect and unchanging." Galileo's telescope showed mountains and craters on the Moon and dark spots moving across the face of the Sun. The heavens were made of real, imperfect places.

Objection 3: "We see no stellar parallax." Galileo's telescope revealed that the Milky Way is made of countless faint stars, far more than anyone had imagined. It was now easy to believe the stars were far enough away that their parallax was simply too small to detect.

Two further discoveries went straight at the geocentric model. In January 1610 Galileo found four moons orbiting Jupiter, clear proof that not everything circles Earth. And later that year he observed Venus going through a full set of phases, including gibbous and nearly full. As Simulation 2.3 shows, Ptolemy's model could not produce those phases at all.

2.7

How science works

The Copernican revolution is a good case study because it shows science happening slowly and messily, with smart people on both sides. Looking back, we can pick out three hallmarks of scientific thinking:

  1. Science explains the world with natural causes. Ptolemy and Copernicus both looked for physical models, not supernatural ones.
  2. Science uses models that make testable predictions. A model earns trust when its predictions match observations, and it must be revised or thrown out when they do not, as Kepler did with his 8-arcminute error.
  3. Science prefers the simpler model. When two models explain the evidence equally well, scientists choose the simpler one, a principle called . Kepler's handful of ellipses beat dozens of circles and epicycles.

These hallmarks are often summarized as the scientific method: observe, ask a question, propose a , make a prediction, test it, and revise. Real science is less tidy than that list suggests, since discoveries can start from accidents, hunches, or new tools like the telescope, but the core idea holds: ideas must face evidence.

What "theory" means in science

In everyday speech, "theory" means a guess ("I have a theory about why the bus is late"). In science, it means nearly the opposite. A is a broad explanation that ties together many facts and has survived repeated, rigorous testing. The theory of gravity and the theory that Earth orbits the Sun are theories in this sense.

Think of it this way

A hypothesis is a bridge design on paper. A scientific theory is a bridge that thousands of trucks have crossed every day for decades. Both can in principle be improved or replaced, but you would not call the tested bridge "just a design."

Science and pseudoscience

Claims that borrow the look of science without following its methods are called . Warning signs include predictions too vague to fail, claims that cannot be tested, and explanations that keep shifting to avoid contrary evidence. Astrology, the belief that the positions of the Sun, Moon, and planets at your birth shape your personality and future, is a common example. It shares roots with astronomy, but when astrological predictions have been tested in controlled studies, they have done no better than chance.

2.8

Two kinds of day

How long is a day? It depends on what you use as your reference. The , from one noon to the next, averages exactly 24 hours; it is the day our clocks keep. But Earth actually completes one full rotation relative to the distant stars in about 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds. That shorter period is the ("sidereal" means "related to the stars").

The difference comes from Earth's orbit. In one day Earth travels about 1° of the way around the Sun. After one full rotation relative to the stars, Earth has shifted along its orbit, so the Sun is no longer in quite the same direction. Earth must turn about 1° more, which takes about 4 minutes, to bring the Sun back to your meridian.

Think of it this way

Picture yourself walking slowly around a campfire while also spinning in place. After one full spin you face the same distant tree again, but because you have walked partway around, you must turn a little extra to face the fire. The tree is a star; the fire is the Sun.

This 4-minute difference has a visible consequence. Stars rise about 4 minutes earlier each night by the clock. That adds up to about 2 hours per month, which is why the constellations overhead at 9 p.m. drift westward through the year, as you saw in Module 1.

2.9

Months, years, and calendars

Months come from the Moon: its cycle of phases (the synodic month) takes about 29.5 days. Years come from the Sun. But which year? The , from one March equinox to the next, lasts about 365.2422 days. The , one orbit relative to the stars, is about 20 minutes longer, because the slow precession of Earth's axis (Module 1) shifts the equinox a little each year. The seasons follow the tropical year, so that is the one calendars track.

The trouble is that 365.2422 is not a whole number. A calendar with exactly 365 days falls behind the seasons by about a quarter of a day each year.

Think of it this way

A calendar is like a watch that runs a little fast or slow. A small error each day barely matters, but it piles up. A leap day is the calendar's way of resetting the watch before the error gets noticeable.

In 46 BCE Julius Caesar introduced the Julian calendar, which adds a every fourth year, making the average year 365.25 days. That is about 11 minutes too long. Over centuries the error grew, and by the 1500s the March equinox was falling around March 11. In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII fixed the problem: that year, October 4 was followed directly by October 15, and the leap-year rule was refined. In today's Gregorian calendar:

  • Years divisible by 4 are leap years,
  • except years divisible by 100, which are not,
  • except years divisible by 400, which are.

So 2000 was a leap year, but 1900 was not and 2100 will not be. Britain and its American colonies did not switch until 1752, which is why George Washington's birthday moved from February 11 to February 22.

2.10

Telling time by the Sun

For most of history, noon was simply the moment the Sun crossed your meridian, and a sundial told the time. That is . It has two problems.

The first is that solar days are not all the same length. Because Earth moves faster in its elliptical orbit near perihelion (Kepler's second law) and because of the tilt of its axis, sundial time runs ahead of or behind a steady clock by up to about 16 minutes, depending on the date. This difference is the . Clocks keep instead, which averages the Sun's motion so every day is exactly 24 hours.

The second problem is that solar time depends on longitude. Noon arrives about 4 minutes later for every degree you go west. When travel was slow, that did not matter. When railroads arrived, it was chaos: each town kept its own local time, and a single rail line could pass through dozens of them. On November 18, 1883, U.S. and Canadian railroads adopted a handful of time zones, and in 1884 an international conference set the prime meridian through Greenwich, England. Today, clocks keep , the mean solar time of each zone's central meridian, referenced to , which is kept by atomic clocks.

Think of it this way

Time zones work like a school bell. Every classroom's own clock might be a few minutes off, but when the bell rings, everyone changes class together. A time zone agrees to use one shared clock instead of each town's slightly different solar time.

Lincroft sits at about 74.1° W, just east of the Eastern time zone's central meridian at 75° W. So the Sun is highest over Brookdale a few minutes before 12:00 standard time, give or take the equation of time. Daylight saving time shifts clocks an hour later still, which is why summer's solar noon comes around 1 p.m.

2.11

A map of the sky, and special places on Earth

Altitude and azimuth describe where something is in your sky right now, but they change from hour to hour and place to place. To give every star a permanent address, astronomers use coordinates fixed to the celestial sphere itself.

You met in Module 1: it is latitude on the sky. The matching east–west coordinate is (RA), the sky's version of longitude. Its zero point is where the Sun crosses the celestial equator at the March equinox. RA is measured eastward in hours, from 0h to 24h, because the sky turns through all of it in one day, about 15° per hour. Sirius, for example, sits at RA 6h 45m, declination −16.7°.

Think of it this way

Imagine Earth's latitude and longitude lines painted on a clear balloon around Earth and blown outward onto the sky. Declination is the painted latitude. Right ascension is the painted longitude, except that the balloon's "Greenwich" is the March equinox point, and the lines are labeled in hours instead of degrees.

Where the Sun can do unusual things

Earth's 23.4° tilt defines four special latitudes. At the (23.4° N), the Sun is directly overhead at noon on the June solstice; the Tropic of Capricorn (23.4° S) gets the same on the December solstice. Between them, in the tropics, the noon Sun is directly overhead twice a year. Syene, Eratosthenes' well town, lies close to the Tropic of Cancer, which is why its well lit up at the solstice.

At the (66.6° N) and the Antarctic Circle (66.6° S), the Sun stays up for a full 24 hours on the summer solstice and never rises on the winter solstice. Closer to the poles, the midnight Sun and polar night last longer, up to six months each at the poles themselves. You can confirm all of this with the seasons simulation in Module 1.

Pause and predict: New Jersey

Can the Sun ever be directly overhead in New Jersey?

Answer: No. At about 40° N, New Jersey is well north of the Tropic of Cancer, so the noon Sun always stays in the southern sky. Its highest point, at the June solstice, is about 73° up.

2.12

Finding your way by the sky

Latitude is easy. As you learned in Module 1, the altitude of the celestial pole equals your latitude. In the Northern Hemisphere, measure the altitude of Polaris and you have it. Navigators could also use the Sun: measure its altitude at noon, look up its declination for that date, and calculate latitude.

Longitude is hard. The sky looks the same from every longitude; it just turns overhead at a different time. Earth rotates 15° every hour, so the difference between your local time and the time at a reference meridian tells you how far east or west you are. If local noon comes 3 hours after noon in Greenwich, you are 45° west of Greenwich. The catch: you need to know the time back in Greenwich, at sea, for weeks, with no way to check it.

Think of it this way

Two friends on a video call, one in New Jersey and one in California, each look at the Sun. For one it is noon; for the other it is 9 a.m. The three-hour gap alone tells them they are 45° of longitude apart. A navigator does the same thing, but the "friend in Greenwich" has to be a clock carried on board.

For centuries, ships' pendulum clocks failed on rolling decks, and errors in longitude wrecked ships. In 1714 the British government offered a huge prize for a practical solution. The winner was a self-taught clockmaker, John Harrison, whose compact kept time to within seconds a day on a voyage to Jamaica in 1761–62.

Today, the does the same job with the same idea: precise timing. About 30 satellites carry atomic clocks and broadcast time signals. Your phone compares signals from at least four satellites to find its position. The clocks are so precise that GPS must correct for Einstein's relativity, a story for Module 7.

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • Ancient cultures around the world tracked the sky to keep calendars, time farming, and navigate.
  • The Greeks sought natural explanations. Aristotle gave evidence that Earth is round, and Eratosthenes measured its circumference from shadow angles.
  • Ptolemy's Earth-centered model explained retrograde motion with epicycles and worked well enough to last 1,500 years.
  • Copernicus put the Sun at the center in 1543, but with circular orbits his model was no more accurate than Ptolemy's.
  • Tycho's precise data let Kepler discover that orbits are ellipses (first law), that planets sweep equal areas in equal times (second law), and that p² = a³ (third law).
  • Galileo answered the objections to a moving Earth and found Jupiter's moons and the full phases of Venus, which Ptolemy's model could not produce.
  • Science seeks natural explanations, tests models against predictions, and prefers simpler models. A scientific theory is a well-tested explanation, not a guess.
  • A solar day (24 h) is about 4 minutes longer than a sidereal day because Earth moves along its orbit each day.
  • The Gregorian calendar's leap-year rules keep the average year within about 26 seconds of the tropical year.
  • Clocks keep standard time, based on mean solar time at each zone's central meridian; sundials show apparent solar time.
  • Right ascension and declination give stars fixed addresses on the sky. The tropics and polar circles are set by Earth's 23.4° tilt.
  • Latitude comes from the altitude of the celestial pole; longitude requires an accurate clock, from Harrison's chronometer to GPS.

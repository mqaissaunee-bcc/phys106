Small Bodies and Other Planetary Systems

The leftovers of planet formation, the asteroids, comets, and icy worlds of the outer solar system, preserve its history and occasionally crash into planets, sometimes with world-changing results. Then we leave the solar system: in just three decades, astronomers have gone from knowing no planets around other stars to cataloging thousands. The module ends with a review and practice midterm for the first half of the course.

Part A

Leftovers of planet formation

Asteroids, meteorites, comets, dwarf planets, and what happens when they hit.

6.1

Asteroids

Asteroids are rocky and metallic leftovers from the inner solar system. The largest, Ceres, is about 940 km across and round enough to count as a dwarf planet; only a handful exceed 300 km, and there are millions smaller than 1 km. Even all together, the asteroids would add up to much less than the mass of our Moon.

Most orbit in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. Others share Jupiter’s orbit in two swarms (the Trojan asteroids), and some, the , cross Earth’s orbit.

Why is there a belt of leftovers instead of a planet? Jupiter’s gravity. Planetesimals in the belt were repeatedly nudged by Jupiter, which stirred up their orbits so they collided too violently to stick together. The nudging continues today: asteroids whose periods would be a simple fraction of Jupiter’s, such as one-half or one-third, get tugged at the same point in every orbit, and their orbits are gradually cleared out. The result is a series of in the belt.

Think of it this way

Push a child on a swing at the same point in every swing and the motion grows. Jupiter pushes asteroids in resonant orbits the same way, until they are pushed out of that orbit altogether.

Spacecraft have visited many asteroids. NASA’s Dawn orbited Vesta and Ceres, finding that Vesta once had lava flows and that Ceres has bright salt deposits from a briny interior. OSIRIS-REx returned samples of the asteroid Bennu in 2023, and the Psyche mission is on its way to a metal-rich asteroid that may be the exposed core of a shattered protoplanet.

6.2

Meteors and meteorites

Three similar words describe three stages. A is a small piece of rock or metal in space. When one enters Earth’s atmosphere at tens of kilometers per second, friction heats it and the surrounding air until they glow: a , or "shooting star." Most burn up completely. A piece that survives and lands is a .

Think of it this way

A meteor is to a meteoroid what a match flare is to the match: the brief streak of light is the event, not the object. Only the rare pieces that make it all the way to the ground earn the name meteorite.

Meteorites are free samples of asteroids, and a few come from the Moon and Mars. They come in two broad types. are unchanged since the solar system formed; they are the source of the 4.56-billion-year age of the solar system (Module 4). Processed meteorites come from bodies large enough to have melted and differentiated: iron meteorites are pieces of shattered metal cores.

are a different phenomenon. They happen when Earth passes through the trail of dust left by a comet. The tiny grains all enter the atmosphere moving in parallel, so their streaks seem to fan out from a single point in the sky, named after the constellation it lies in: the Perseids in August, the Geminids in December.

6.3

Comets

A comet begins as a , a lump of ice mixed with rock and dust, typically a few kilometers across and as dark as charcoal. Far from the Sun it is frozen and invisible. As it falls inward, sunlight warms it and its ices sublimate (turn directly from solid to gas), releasing dust. The gas and dust form a glowing around the nucleus, which can grow larger than a planet.

Two tails form, and both point away from the Sun. The straight, bluish is gas blown directly outward by the solar wind. The broader, curved dust tail is pushed more gently by sunlight and trails along the comet’s path. Tails can stretch for over 100 million kilometers, yet contain so little material that you could fit an entire tail’s worth of gas in a suitcase.

Think of it this way

A comet’s tail behaves like the flag on a boat in a strong crosswind: it streams in the direction the wind blows, not the direction the boat moves. For a comet, the "wind" blows outward from the Sun.

Comets come from the two icy reservoirs of Module 4. Short-period comets (orbits under about 200 years) mostly come from the Kuiper belt and orbit near the plane of the planets. Long-period comets fall in from the Oort cloud from every direction. The European Space Agency’s Rosetta orbited Comet 67P from 2014 to 2016 and landed a probe on its surface, finding a rubber-duck-shaped nucleus rich in organic molecules.

6.4

Pluto and the Kuiper belt

For decades after its 1930 discovery, Pluto seemed like a misfit: a small, icy world on an eccentric, tilted orbit beyond Neptune. Since 1992, astronomers have found thousands of similar icy bodies orbiting in the Kuiper belt. In 2005 they found Eris, nearly Pluto’s size, which forced a decision about what counts as a planet. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union classified Pluto and Eris as : round, but not dominant in their orbital zones.

Pluto is still a fascinating world. NASA’s New Horizons flew past in 2015 and found mountains of water ice as tall as the Rockies, a vast plain of nitrogen ice that slowly churns, a thin nitrogen atmosphere with layers of haze, and a large moon, Charon, half Pluto’s size. In 2019 New Horizons passed Arrokoth, a small Kuiper belt object shaped like a snowman: two planetesimals that gently merged 4.5 billion years ago, a snapshot of planet formation frozen in time.

Common misconception

“Pluto got demoted because it isn’t important.” The reclassification reflected a discovery, not a demotion: Pluto is the best-studied example of a whole new class of worlds, the icy dwarf planets of the Kuiper belt, which outnumber the eight planets.

6.5

Cosmic collisions

Impacts didn’t stop with the heavy bombardment; they just became rarer. In 1994, the fragments of Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 slammed into Jupiter, leaving dark scars larger than Earth. On Earth, about 200 impact craters have been identified.

Sixty-six million years ago, an asteroid about 10 km across struck what is now the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico, creating the 180-km-wide Chicxulub crater. The evidence is a worldwide layer of clay rich in iridium (an element common in asteroids but rare in Earth’s crust), shocked quartz, and glassy droplets, all dated to the same moment. The impact triggered fires, a global darkening, and the mass extinction that ended the age of the dinosaurs, clearing the way for mammals, and eventually us.

Smaller impacts are more common. In 1908 an object about 50 m across exploded over Tunguska, Siberia, flattening 2,000 km² of forest. In 2013 a roughly 20-m asteroid burst over Chelyabinsk, Russia, shattering windows and injuring about 1,500 people.

Think of it this way

Impact risk works like earthquakes or floods: small events are frequent, catastrophic ones are rare. A Chelyabinsk-sized airburst happens about once a century; a Chicxulub-sized impact about once every 100 million years.

Today, survey telescopes track near-Earth asteroids, and none of the known large ones is on a collision course. In 2022 NASA’s DART spacecraft deliberately crashed into the small asteroid Dimorphos and shortened its orbit around its companion by 33 minutes, the first test of .

6.6

The challenge of finding planets

Planets orbiting other stars are called . They are extraordinarily hard to see directly for two reasons. A Sun-like star is about a billion times brighter than an Earth-like planet in visible light, and from light-years away the planet sits a tiny fraction of an arcsecond from its star, lost in the glare.

Think of it this way

Imaging an Earth-like planet next to its star is like trying to spot a firefly hovering beside a stadium floodlight, from hundreds of kilometers away.

is possible for young, hot, giant planets far from their stars, especially in infrared light, where the brightness contrast is smaller. Special instruments block the starlight. But most exoplanets have been found indirectly, by watching how they affect their stars.

6.7

The Doppler (radial velocity) method

A planet doesn’t simply orbit its star; both orbit their shared . Because the star is so much more massive, it moves in a tiny circle, but it does move. As it swings toward and away from us, its spectral lines shift back and forth by the Doppler effect (Module 3).

Think of it this way

Watch two people of very different weights spinning while holding hands: the big person barely circles, but they do wobble. You could detect the small partner, even in the dark, just by watching the big one wobble.

The size of the wobble reveals the planet’s mass (strictly, a minimum mass, since we may see the orbit at an angle), and the time for one cycle gives its orbital period. Jupiter makes the Sun wobble at about 13 m/s; Earth, only 9 cm/s, about walking speed for an ant. In 1995 Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz used this method to find 51 Pegasi b, the first planet around a Sun-like star, a Jupiter-mass planet orbiting in just 4.2 days. They shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics.

6.8

The transit method

If a planet’s orbit is lined up edge-on from our point of view, the planet passes in front of its star once every orbit, blocking a little of its light: a . The dip in brightness tells us the planet’s size relative to the star, and the time between dips gives its orbital period.

fraction of light blocked = (planet radius ÷ star radius)²
Think of it this way

A moth flying in front of a distant porch light: you can’t see the moth, but if you measured the light carefully, you would notice a slight, regular dimming every time it crossed. The bigger the moth, the bigger the dip.

NASA’s Kepler space telescope (2009–2018) stared at about 150,000 stars and found thousands of planets this way; its successor TESS surveys nearly the whole sky. When starlight passes through a transiting planet’s atmosphere, some wavelengths are absorbed, so transits also let us study exoplanet atmospheres. In 2022 the James Webb Space Telescope detected carbon dioxide in the atmosphere of the giant planet WASP-39b.

6.9

What we have learned

In September 2025 NASA’s exoplanet archive passed 6,000 confirmed exoplanets, with thousands more candidates awaiting confirmation. With both a transit (size) and Doppler measurement (mass), astronomers can calculate a planet’s density and tell whether it is rocky or gaseous. The results were full of surprises:

  • : giant planets orbiting closer to their stars than Mercury is to the Sun, with surface temperatures over 1,000 °C.
  • : planets between Earth and Neptune in size, the most common kind in the galaxy, yet absent from our solar system.
  • Eccentric orbits: many planets follow stretched, elongated orbits, unlike the near-circles of our planets.
  • Crowded systems: the TRAPPIST-1 system has seven Earth-size planets packed within a distance smaller than Mercury’s orbit, several of them in the star’s habitable zone.

The is the range of distances from a star where a planet with an Earth-like atmosphere could have liquid water on its surface. It lies closer to dim stars and farther from bright ones. The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, has an Earth-mass planet in its habitable zone.

6.10

Revisiting the nebular theory

Hot Jupiters were a shock. According to the nebular theory, giant planets form beyond the frost line, where ice is plentiful. How could Jupiter-size planets end up closer to their stars than Mercury?

The answer is . Interactions between a young planet and the gas disk around it can make the planet spiral inward, or outward, over time. Gravitational encounters among planets can fling some onto eccentric orbits or out of the system entirely. Our own solar system probably saw migration too: models suggest Jupiter and Saturn wandered early on, and that Uranus and Neptune formed closer to the Sun and moved outward, scattering icy planetesimals into the Kuiper belt and Oort cloud.

Think of it this way

The nebular theory was like a recipe that had only ever been tested in one kitchen. Cooking it in thousands of other kitchens revealed that the same ingredients and steps can produce very different results depending on small details. The recipe survived; it just needed notes added.

This is science working as it should (Module 2): new evidence did not overturn the nebular theory, which still explains how planets form. It revealed processes the theory hadn’t included, and the theory was refined. It also showed that our solar system, with no hot Jupiters or super-Earths, is only one of many possible outcomes.

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • Asteroids are rocky and metallic leftovers, mostly between Mars and Jupiter; Jupiter’s gravity prevented a planet from forming there and carved the Kirkwood gaps.
  • Meteoroids become meteors in the atmosphere and meteorites on the ground; primitive meteorites preserve the solar system’s original material and age.
  • Comets are icy nuclei that grow a coma and two tails near the Sun, both pointing away from it. They come from the Kuiper belt and the Oort cloud.
  • Pluto is a dwarf planet, one of many icy bodies in the Kuiper belt, revealed as a complex world by New Horizons.
  • Impacts still happen. One caused the extinction of the dinosaurs 66 million years ago; DART showed we can nudge an asteroid.
  • Exoplanets are hard to see directly. The Doppler method measures a star’s wobble (planet mass and period); the transit method measures dips in brightness (planet size).
  • More than 6,000 exoplanets are known, including hot Jupiters and super-Earths. The habitable zone is where liquid surface water is possible.
  • Planetary migration explains hot Jupiters, refining the nebular theory rather than replacing it.

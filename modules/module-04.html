The Solar System and Its Origin

A guided tour of our planetary system, followed by a detective story. The planets are not arranged at random: their orbits, sizes, and compositions follow clear patterns. Those patterns are clues, and the nebular theory explains nearly all of them with one idea: the solar system formed from a collapsing cloud of gas and dust 4.6 billion years ago.

Part A

A tour of the solar system

What is out there, and the patterns that stand out.

4.1

Patterns in the solar system

Look at the solar system as a whole, and four features jump out. Any explanation of where the solar system came from has to account for all of them.

  1. Orderly motion. All the planets orbit the Sun in the same direction, in nearly circular orbits that lie close to one plane. Most also rotate in that same direction, as does the Sun, and so do most large moons around their planets.
  2. Two kinds of planets. The four inner are small, rocky, and dense, with few moons and no rings. The four outer are huge, low in density, made mostly of hydrogen, helium, and hydrogen compounds, with many moons and ring systems.
  3. Swarms of small bodies. Rocky orbit mostly between Mars and Jupiter; icy come mostly from the far outer regions.
  4. Notable exceptions. A few oddities break the patterns: Uranus is tipped on its side, Venus spins backward, and Earth has an unusually large moon.
Think of it this way

A detective arriving at a scene looks for patterns first, then for the details that don’t fit. The patterns point to the general story; the oddities point to specific events. The four features above play the same roles for planetary scientists: three patterns to explain, plus a list of exceptions that need their own explanations.

4.2

The Sun at the center

The Sun holds more than 99.8% of the solar system’s total mass. It is an ordinary star: a ball of hot, electrically charged gas called plasma, 109 times Earth’s diameter, held together by its own gravity. Its surface is about 5,800 K; its core is about 15 million K.

The Sun shines because of in its core, where hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium and convert a little mass into a great deal of energy (E = mc², Module 3). It has been doing this steadily for 4.6 billion years. Its gravity governs every planet’s orbit, its light warms every world, and its constant outflow of charged particles, the , sweeps through the entire planetary system. You will study the Sun in detail in Module 9.

Think of it this way

If the solar system were a family, the Sun would outweigh everyone else combined by a factor of about 700. Jupiter would be the only other member whose weight really registers; Earth would be a speck by comparison.

4.3

The terrestrial worlds

Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun, a heavily cratered, airless world with a huge iron core that makes up much of its volume. Days are hot enough to melt lead (about 430 °C) and nights plunge to −180 °C. It rotates exactly three times for every two orbits.

Venus is nearly Earth’s twin in size but a hellish place. A crushing carbon-dioxide atmosphere, 90 times the pressure of Earth’s, traps heat in an extreme , giving it the hottest surface of any planet, about 465 °C, day and night. It rotates slowly backward, once every 243 Earth days.

Earth is the only world known to have surface oceans of liquid water, an oxygen-rich atmosphere, plate tectonics, and life. Its Moon is remarkably large compared with its planet.

Mars is about half Earth’s diameter, a cold desert with a thin carbon-dioxide atmosphere. It has the largest volcano in the solar system (Olympus Mons, about three times as tall as Mount Everest) and a canyon system (Valles Marineris) that would stretch across the United States. Dry riverbeds and minerals that form in water show that liquid water once flowed there.

Think of it this way

Think of the four terrestrial planets as siblings who grew up in different climates. Built from the same basic ingredients, they ended up very different because of their size and their distance from the Sun, the story of Module 5.

4.4

The jovian worlds

Jupiter is the giant: 11 times Earth’s diameter and 318 times its mass, more than twice the mass of all the other planets combined. It has no solid surface; its colorful cloud bands sit atop a deep atmosphere of hydrogen and helium. The Great Red Spot is a storm larger than Earth that has raged for centuries. Its four large Galilean moons are worlds in their own right (Module 5).

Saturn is almost as large but only about a third as massive, so its average density is less than water’s. Its spectacular rings are made of countless chunks of water ice, from dust grains to boulders, spread in a disk hundreds of thousands of kilometers wide but typically only tens of meters thick.

Think of it this way

If Saturn’s rings were scaled down to the width of a football field, they would be thinner than a sheet of paper.

Uranus and Neptune are the ice giants, about four times Earth’s diameter, made mostly of hydrogen compounds (water, methane, ammonia) with thinner hydrogen–helium atmospheres. Methane absorbs red light, giving both their blue-green color. Uranus is tipped on its side, with an axis tilt of 98°, so its poles take turns facing the Sun for 42 years at a time. Neptune has the fastest winds in the solar system, and its largest moon, Triton, orbits backward, a clue that it was captured.

Common misconception

“Saturn is the only planet with rings.” All four jovian planets have ring systems. Saturn’s are simply the brightest, because they are made of highly reflective water ice. Jupiter’s, Uranus’s, and Neptune’s are dark and faint.

4.5

Asteroids, comets, and dwarf planets

Countless small bodies orbit the Sun too. They are leftovers from the solar system’s formation, and their locations turn out to be important clues.

The , between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter, holds most known asteroids: rocky and metallic bodies from a few meters to about 940 km across (Ceres). Despite what movies show, the belt is mostly empty space; spacecraft pass through without special maneuvers.

Beyond Neptune lies the , a flattened ring of icy bodies extending from about 30 to 55 AU. Pluto is one of its largest members. Much farther out, perhaps 50,000 AU or more, is the , a vast, roughly spherical swarm of perhaps a trillion comets surrounding the solar system. When a comet falls inward toward the Sun, its ices vaporize into a glowing coma and a tail that always points away from the Sun.

In 2006 the International Astronomical Union defined a as a round object that orbits the Sun but has not cleared its orbit of other objects. Pluto, Eris, Ceres, Haumea, and Makemake are recognized dwarf planets. Pluto was reclassified not because it changed, but because astronomers discovered that it is one of many similar Kuiper belt objects.

Think of it this way

Think of the asteroids and comets as the construction debris left over after a building is finished: the rubble tells you what materials were used and how the job went. Rocky debris sits in the warm inner region; icy debris sits in the cold outer region, just as the formation story in Part B predicts.

4.6

How we explore the solar system

Telescopes on Earth taught us a great deal, but most of what we know about other worlds comes from robotic spacecraft. They come in four types:

  • Flybys pass a world once and continue on, like Voyager 1 and 2, which toured the outer planets in the 1970s and 1980s and are now in interstellar space.
  • Orbiters go into orbit for long-term study, like Juno at Jupiter and the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.
  • Landers, rovers, and probes touch down or dive in, like the Perseverance rover on Mars and the Huygens probe that landed on Saturn’s moon Titan.
  • Sample-return missions bring material back to Earth for laboratory study, like the Apollo missions, Japan’s Hayabusa2, and NASA’s OSIRIS-REx, which returned pieces of the asteroid Bennu in 2023.

Spacecraft often use , swinging past a planet to steal a little of its orbital energy and change speed and direction without fuel.

Think of it this way

A gravity assist is like jumping onto a moving merry-go-round and jumping off the other side: you leave faster than you arrived, having borrowed a tiny bit of the ride’s motion. The planet slows by an immeasurably small amount.

Part B

How the solar system formed

The nebular theory: one idea that explains the patterns.

4.7

The nebular theory

The leading explanation for the origin of the solar system is the : the Sun and planets formed together, about 4.6 billion years ago, from the gravitational collapse of an interstellar cloud of gas and dust, the .

The idea dates back to the 1700s, but it earned the status of a scientific theory because it has passed many tests. It explains the four major features of Section 4.1, and its predictions keep being confirmed: telescopes now show young stars surrounded by flattened, spinning disks of gas and dust, and we have found thousands of planetary systems around other stars (Module 6).

Where did the cloud’s material come from? About 98% of it was hydrogen and helium made in the Big Bang. The remaining 2%, everything from carbon and oxygen to iron, was made inside earlier generations of stars and scattered into space when they died. The atoms in your body were forged in stars that lived and died before the Sun was born.

Think of it this way

The galaxy recycles like a compost heap: old stars return enriched material to space, and new stars and planets form from the mix. Each generation starts with slightly more heavy elements than the last.

4.8

Heating, spinning, and flattening

As the solar nebula collapsed under its own gravity, three things happened, each following from the conservation laws of Module 3.

It heated up. As gas fell inward, gravitational potential energy turned into thermal energy. The center became hottest, eventually hot enough to become the Sun.

It spun faster. The cloud started with a slight rotation. By conservation of angular momentum, shrinking made it spin faster, just like the figure skater pulling in her arms.

It flattened. Colliding gas clumps cancel out one another’s up-and-down motions, while the spin keeps material from falling straight inward in the plane of rotation. The result was a spinning .

Think of it this way

Watch a pizza maker toss spinning dough: the round lump flattens into a disk because of its spin. A collapsing, spinning cloud flattens for a related reason, which is why the planets all orbit in nearly the same plane and the same direction.

4.9

Condensation and the frost line

The disk was hottest near the center and coldest far out. As it cooled, some materials from gas into solid grains, but only where it was cool enough for each one. The materials fell into four groups:

The dividing line where it became cold enough for hydrogen compounds to freeze into ice is the , which lay between the present-day orbits of Mars and Jupiter. Inside it, only metal and rock (just 0.6% of the material) could form solid grains. Beyond it, ices added more than twice as much solid material.

Think of it this way

Breathe on a cold window and water droplets form; breathe on a warm one and nothing happens. The water vapor is the same; only the temperature decides whether it becomes solid (or liquid). The solar nebula was a giant version of that window, warm near the Sun and cold far away.

4.10

Building the planets

Once solid grains existed, they stuck together. Tiny grains clumped into pebbles, pebbles into boulders, and boulders into , bodies kilometers across whose gravity could pull in more material. This growth is called . Collisions and mergers eventually built a few large bodies in each region.

Inside the frost line, the scarce metal and rock limited how large planetesimals could grow. The result: small, dense, rocky planets. Their compositions reflect the temperatures where they formed; Mercury, closest to the Sun, has the largest share of metal.

Beyond the frost line, abundant ice let planetesimals grow bigger, to perhaps 10 times Earth’s mass. That was big enough for their gravity to pull in the surrounding hydrogen and helium gas, a process called . The captured gas swelled them into giants. The disks of gas around them spun and flattened like miniature solar nebulas, forming their many regular moons.

Clearing the nebula. Within about 10 million years, the young Sun’s strong solar wind blew the remaining gas away. Planet formation effectively stopped; whatever had not been captured by then was gone. Uranus and Neptune, farther out where material was sparser and orbits slower, captured less gas than Jupiter and Saturn before the clearing.

Think of it this way

Building a planet by accretion is like rolling a snowball: once it is big enough, it picks up more with every turn. Beyond the frost line there was far more “snow” to pick up, so the snowballs grew large enough to start gathering the air itself.

4.11

Explaining the exceptions

The nebular theory explains the patterns; the exceptions come from specific events in the chaotic early solar system.

Leftover planetesimals. Rocky planetesimals that never joined a planet remain as asteroids; icy ones remain as comets. Jupiter’s gravity stirred up the asteroid belt and kept a planet from forming there. The outer planets flung many icy planetesimals outward, populating the Oort cloud, or inward toward the inner planets.

. For several hundred million years, leftover planetesimals rained down on the young planets, leaving the craters we still see on the Moon and Mercury. Icy impactors from beyond the frost line likely delivered much of Earth’s water.

Captured moons. Some small, irregular moons, like the two tiny moons of Mars and Neptune’s backward-orbiting Triton, were probably passing bodies captured by a planet’s gravity.

Giant impacts. The strongest evidence says our Moon formed when a Mars-sized body slammed into the young Earth, blasting out material that reassembled in orbit. This explains why the Moon is so large compared with Earth, why its composition matches Earth’s outer layers, and why it has very little iron. Similar violent collisions may have tipped Uranus onto its side and flipped Venus’s spin.

Think of it this way

Think of the early solar system as a demolition derby after the main race: most cars (planetesimals) had already merged into a few big winners, but the leftover cars kept crashing into them for a long time. A few of those crashes were big enough to change the winners permanently.

4.12

How old is the solar system?

We know the solar system’s age from . Some atoms are radioactive: their nuclei spontaneously change into other nuclei. Each radioactive isotope has a , the time it takes for half of the atoms in a sample to decay. Potassium-40, for example, decays into argon-40 with a half-life of 1.25 billion years.

When a rock solidifies, it traps its radioactive atoms. From then on, the decay products build up inside it. By measuring the ratio of “daughter” atoms to remaining “parent” atoms, scientists can calculate how many half-lives have passed, and so how long ago the rock formed.

Think of it this way

Think of an hourglass that started full on one side. You can’t see the moment it was turned over, but by comparing how much sand has fallen with how much is left, you can tell how long it has been running, provided you know how fast the sand flows. Radioactive decay is an hourglass whose flow rate never changes.

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • The solar system shows four major features: orderly orbits and spins, two kinds of planets, swarms of small bodies, and a few notable exceptions.
  • Terrestrial planets are small, dense, and rocky with few moons; jovian planets are huge, low-density, gas- and ice-rich, with rings and many moons.
  • The Sun holds over 99.8% of the mass and shines by nuclear fusion.
  • Asteroids fill the belt between Mars and Jupiter; icy bodies fill the Kuiper belt beyond Neptune and the distant Oort cloud. Pluto is a dwarf planet in the Kuiper belt.
  • Spacecraft explore by flyby, orbit, landing, and sample return, often using gravity assists.
  • The nebular theory says the solar system formed 4.6 billion years ago from a collapsing cloud that heated, spun faster, and flattened into a disk.
  • Inside the frost line only metal and rock condensed, building small terrestrial planets; beyond it, ices allowed large cores that captured hydrogen and helium gas.
  • The solar wind cleared leftover gas; leftover planetesimals became asteroids and comets, and bombarded the young planets.
  • Captures and giant impacts explain exceptions, including Earth’s large Moon.
  • Radiometric dating uses half-lives to measure ages; the oldest meteorites date the solar system to 4.56 billion years.

Galaxies and Their Evolution

A century ago, astronomers argued over whether the Milky Way was the whole universe. Today we know it is one of hundreds of billions of galaxies, and that the universe is expanding. This module explains how we measure distances across billions of light-years, what Hubble’s law reveals, and how galaxies have grown, collided, and been shaped by the monster black holes at their centers.

  9. Explain what quasars and active galactic nuclei are and how supermassive black holes power them.
  10. Describe the large-scale structure of the universe and how clusters reveal dark matter.

Part A

Galaxies and the scale of the universe

Classifying galaxies and measuring the distance to them.

12.1

Islands of stars

In 1920, astronomers held a "Great Debate." Were the fuzzy spiral nebulae clouds inside the Milky Way, or separate "island universes" far beyond it? The answer came in 1924, when Edwin Hubble, using the new 100-inch telescope on Mount Wilson, found Cepheid variable stars in the Andromeda "nebula." Their pulsations revealed that Andromeda is millions of light-years away, far outside the Milky Way: a galaxy in its own right.

We now know the observable universe contains hundreds of billions of galaxies, perhaps up to two trillion counting the faintest. Galaxies range from dwarfs with a few million stars to giants with more than a trillion.

Think of it this way

Before 1924 it was as if we had only ever seen our own town and assumed it was the whole country. Hubble’s discovery was like learning that the lights on the horizon were other cities, each as big as ours, scattered across a continent.

12.2

Types of galaxies

Hubble sorted galaxies by shape into a scheme still used today:

  • have a flat disk with spiral arms, a central bulge, and a halo. Their disks are rich in gas and dust and full of young, blue stars. About half of spirals, including the Milky Way, are barred spirals, with a bar of stars through the center.
  • are smooth, round or egg-shaped swarms of mostly old, reddish stars, with little cool gas and almost no new star formation. They include both the smallest dwarfs and the largest giants.
  • Lenticular galaxies (S0) have a disk and bulge but no spiral arms and little gas, in between spirals and ellipticals.
  • have no clear shape. Many are small and gas-rich, like the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds.

Galaxies gather into , like our Local Group of about 80 mostly small galaxies, and of hundreds to thousands of galaxies. Ellipticals are most common in the crowded centers of clusters, a clue to how they formed (Section 12.8).

12.3

The cosmic distance ladder

No single method measures every distance. Astronomers use a chain of techniques, the , where each rung is calibrated by the one below it:

  1. Radar measures distances within the solar system, including the AU.
  2. Parallax (Module 9) measures distances to stars in our part of the galaxy.
  3. Main-sequence fitting compares star clusters’ H-R diagrams to find their distances.
  4. , pulsating supergiants whose period reveals their luminosity, reach nearby galaxies.
  5. White dwarf supernovae (Module 11), standard candles bright enough to see billions of light-years away.
  6. Hubble’s law, calibrated by all of the above, gives distances to the most remote galaxies.
Think of it this way

The distance ladder is like measuring a long highway with tools of different sizes: a ruler for your driveway, a tape measure for the block, the car’s odometer for the next town, and a map scale for the whole state. Each tool is checked against the previous one where their ranges overlap.

The Cepheid rung rests on the work of Henrietta Swan Leavitt, one of the Harvard "computers" (Module 9). In 1912 she found that Cepheids with longer pulsation periods are more luminous: the . That turned Cepheids into standard candles and made Hubble’s discoveries possible.

12.4

Hubble’s law and the expanding universe

In 1929 Edwin Hubble, building on redshift measurements by Vesto Slipher, compared galaxies’ distances with their speeds from Doppler shifts (Module 3). Nearly all galaxies were moving away from us, and the farther away, the faster. This is :

velocity = H₀ × distance

where H₀, the , is about 70 km/s per megaparsec (1 megaparsec = 3.26 million light-years). A galaxy 100 Mpc away recedes at about 7,000 km/s. Different measurement methods currently give slightly different values, from about 67 to 73; this "Hubble tension" is an active research question.

Hubble’s law means the universe is . Galaxies are not flying through space away from us; space itself is stretching, carrying galaxies apart. And the pattern would look the same from any galaxy: there is no center.

Think of it this way

Bake raisin bread (Module 1) or draw dots on a balloon and blow it up. Every raisin or dot moves away from every other one, and ones farther apart separate faster. No raisin is at the center, yet each one sees the same Hubble’s law.

12.5

Using Hubble’s law

Once calibrated, Hubble’s law becomes the top rung of the distance ladder: measure a galaxy’s from its spectrum, convert to a velocity, and divide by H₀. For light from distant galaxies, the redshift isn’t really a Doppler shift from motion through space; the light’s wavelength has been stretched by the expansion of space during its journey.

Running the expansion backward gives an estimate of the universe’s age. If galaxies have always been moving apart at their current speeds, they were all together about 1/H₀ ago, roughly 14 billion years for H₀ = 70. Careful models that account for how the expansion rate has changed give 13.8 billion years (Module 13).

Think of it this way

If a car is 140 km from home and driving away at 70 km/h, you can guess it left 2 hours ago, assuming it drove at a steady speed. Hubble’s law lets astronomers make the same kind of estimate for the whole universe.

Part B

How galaxies change

Galaxy birth, collisions, quasars, and the cosmic web.

12.6

Looking back in time

Because light takes time to reach us (Module 1), distant galaxies appear as they were when their light set out. A deep telescope image is a slice through cosmic history. The Hubble Ultra Deep Field revealed about 10,000 galaxies in a patch of sky smaller than a grain of sand held at arm’s length, many seen as they were when the universe was less than a billion years old.

The James Webb Space Telescope, which sees the infrared light that the expansion has stretched from young galaxies’ ultraviolet and visible light, has found galaxies seen as they were less than 300 million years after the Big Bang, brighter and more developed than many astronomers expected. Comparing galaxies at different distances is like comparing photos of people at different ages: we can’t watch one galaxy grow up, but we can see many at different stages.

Think of it this way

Imagine trying to learn how trees grow by walking through a forest for one afternoon. You can’t watch any single tree grow, but you can see seedlings, saplings, and old giants side by side. Deep images give astronomers the same view of galaxies across billions of years.

12.7

How galaxies form

The leading model says galaxies formed inside clumps of dark matter. After the Big Bang, the universe was almost perfectly smooth, but slightly denser regions pulled in more matter by gravity. Dark matter collapsed into , and ordinary gas fell into them, cooled, and formed stars. These first protogalactic clouds merged and grew into the galaxies we see today.

Why do some galaxies become spirals and others ellipticals? Two factors seem to matter. A cloud that had more spin (angular momentum) and formed its stars gradually settled into a flat, rotating disk, like the solar nebula. A cloud that formed its stars quickly, before the gas could settle, kept them in a round swarm. And collisions (next section) can turn spirals into ellipticals.

Think of it this way

It’s like pouring pancake batter onto a griddle. Pour slowly while swirling and it spreads into a thin disk; dump it all at once and it stays in a thick lump. Galaxies that formed stars slowly while spinning became flat spirals.

12.8

When galaxies collide

Galaxies are big compared with the distances between them, so collisions are common, especially in groups and clusters. When two galaxies collide, their stars almost never hit each other; stars are so far apart that it’s like two swarms of bees passing through each other. But their gravity rearranges them dramatically, flinging out long tails of stars, and their gas clouds do collide, compressing and triggering with star formation rates hundreds of times the Milky Way’s. A merger of two spirals can scramble their orderly disks into an elliptical galaxy.

The Milky Way itself is on a collision course. Andromeda is approaching at about 110 km/s. Recent studies using Hubble and Gaia data suggest roughly even odds that the two galaxies will merge within the next 10 billion years; if they do, they will likely end up as a giant elliptical galaxy. The Sun would almost certainly survive, just relocated.

Think of it this way

A galaxy collision is less like a car crash and more like two marching bands walking through each other: the band members rarely bump, but the formations are thrown into complete disarray.

12.9

Quasars and active galactic nuclei

In the 1960s astronomers found radio sources that looked like faint stars but had enormous redshifts, putting them billions of light-years away. To be visible at that distance, these must be incredibly luminous, some outshining a thousand Milky Ways. Yet their brightness can change in days, which means the light comes from a region no bigger than a few light-days across, about the size of our solar system.

Think of it this way

If a whole stadium’s lights flicker in perfect unison, you know they are controlled from one small switchboard, not hundreds of separate switches. A quasar’s rapid flickering tells us its enormous power comes from a tiny central engine.

Only one thing can pack that much power into so small a space: a , millions to billions of times the Sun’s mass, swallowing gas from an accretion disk. Infalling matter heats up and radiates up to about 10% of its mass-energy, far more efficiently than fusion. Many also launch jets of particles at nearly the speed of light, extending far beyond their galaxies. Quasars and their milder cousins, including Seyfert galaxies and radio galaxies, are all .

12.10

Supermassive black holes and their galaxies

Quasars were most common when the universe was a few billion years old, and nearby galaxies rarely host them today. Where did they go? The answer seems to be that the black holes are still there, in the centers of nearly every large galaxy, including our own Sagittarius A* (Module 11), but most have run out of fuel and gone quiet.

A remarkable pattern links black holes to their galaxies: the mass of a galaxy’s central black hole is about 0.1–0.2% of the mass of its bulge. This suggests black holes and galaxies grew together. Energy blasting out from an active black hole can heat and expel gas, shutting down star formation, a process called feedback. The black hole may help regulate how big its galaxy can grow.

Think of it this way

A supermassive black hole acts like a furnace’s thermostat for its galaxy: when it gets fed and "switches on," its output heats the surroundings and cuts off its own fuel supply, keeping the whole system in balance.

12.11

Clusters, dark matter, and the cosmic web

Galaxy clusters are the largest objects held together by gravity, and they are a lab for measuring mass. Three independent methods agree:

  • Galaxy speeds: galaxies in clusters move so fast that the clusters would fly apart unless they contain far more mass than we see (first noticed by Fritz Zwicky in the 1930s).
  • Hot gas: clusters are filled with gas at tens of millions of degrees, glowing in X-rays. Only strong gravity can hold such hot gas in place.
  • Gravitational lensing (Module 7): clusters bend light from more distant galaxies into arcs, and the amount of bending measures their total mass.

All three show that clusters, like galaxies, are dominated by dark matter, roughly five to six times more than ordinary matter. On the largest scales, galaxies and clusters are arranged in a vast : long filaments and sheets of galaxies surrounding enormous empty hundreds of millions of light-years across. This pattern grew from tiny ripples in the early universe, the subject of Module 13.

Think of it this way

The cosmic web looks like the foam in a glass of beer or a kitchen sponge: thin walls and threads of galaxies surrounding huge empty bubbles.

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • In 1924 Hubble used Cepheids to show Andromeda is a separate galaxy; the universe holds hundreds of billions of galaxies.
  • Galaxies are spirals (including barred spirals), ellipticals, lenticulars, or irregulars, and gather into groups and clusters.
  • The distance ladder links radar, parallax, main-sequence fitting, Cepheids (Leavitt’s period–luminosity relation), white dwarf supernovae, and Hubble’s law.
  • Hubble’s law, v = H₀d with H₀ ≈ 70 km/s/Mpc, shows the universe is expanding, with no center; 1/H₀ gives an age of about 14 billion years.
  • Deep images show galaxies as they were billions of years ago, back to a few hundred million years after the Big Bang.
  • Galaxies formed as gas fell into dark matter halos; spin and star formation rate helped decide between spirals and ellipticals.
  • Collisions trigger starbursts and can turn spirals into ellipticals; the Milky Way and Andromeda may merge within 10 billion years.
  • Quasars and other active galactic nuclei are powered by supermassive black holes swallowing gas; they were most common early in cosmic history.
  • Central black hole masses track their galaxies’ bulges, suggesting they grew together.
  • Galaxy speeds, hot gas, and lensing show that clusters are dominated by dark matter; galaxies lie along a cosmic web of filaments and voids.

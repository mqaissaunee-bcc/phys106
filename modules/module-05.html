Worlds Up Close

Why is Earth alive with volcanoes, oceans, and air while the Moon is a frozen, airless relic? Why is Venus hot enough to melt lead and Mars a freeze-dried desert? And how can small, icy moons of Jupiter and Saturn hide oceans beneath their surfaces? This module compares the worlds of our solar system and finds that a few simple factors, above all size and distance from the Sun, explain most of their differences.

Part A

Planetary geology

What is inside the terrestrial worlds, and what shapes their surfaces.

5.1

Inside the terrestrial worlds

The five rocky worlds of the inner solar system, Mercury, Venus, Earth, the Moon, and Mars, share the same basic interior structure, arranged in layers by density:

  • a of the densest material, mostly iron and nickel;
  • a of rock of moderate density;
  • a thin of the least dense rock, such as granite and basalt.

Geologists also group layers by strength. The cool, rigid outer layer, the crust plus the top of the mantle, is the . Below it, warmer rock flows slowly, like very thick putty, over millions of years.

The layers formed by : when the young worlds were hot enough to melt, dense metals sank to the center and lighter rock floated to the top.

Think of it this way

Shake a bottle of oil-and-vinegar salad dressing and set it down: within minutes the denser vinegar settles to the bottom and the oil floats on top. A molten planet sorts itself the same way, with iron sinking and rock rising.

5.2

Internal heat: why size matters

Geological activity, volcanoes and moving plates, is driven by internal heat. The young worlds got that heat from three sources: the energy of accretion as planetesimals slammed together, the energy released as heavy metals sank during differentiation, and the ongoing decay of radioactive elements in their rock, which still supplies most of Earth’s internal heat today.

Hot rock in the mantle rises, cools, and sinks again in slow loops called . That churning pushes the lithosphere around and feeds volcanoes. But every world is steadily losing its heat to space, and the rate at which it cools depends above all on its size.

Think of it this way

Take a big baked potato and a small one out of the oven together. The small one is cool enough to eat in minutes; the big one stays hot inside much longer. Heat is stored in the whole volume but escapes only through the skin, and a small object has far more skin for its size.

The same reasoning explains magnetic fields. A world generates a global magnetic field when it has a molten, electrically conducting core that is churning (convecting) and a reasonably fast rotation. Earth has all three. The Moon and Mars have cooled too much; Venus has a hot interior but rotates very slowly. Earth’s magnetic field creates a protective bubble, the , that deflects the solar wind.

5.3

Four processes that shape surfaces

Every feature on a rocky surface comes from one of four processes:

  1. : asteroids and comets blast out bowl-shaped craters, typically about 10 times wider than the impactor.
  2. : molten rock (magma) erupts onto the surface, building volcanoes and lava plains. Volcanoes also release gases that form atmospheres.
  3. : forces within the lithosphere stretch, compress, and break the surface, raising mountains and cracking valleys.
  4. : wind, water, and ice wear down and move material, carving canyons and building up sediment.

Craters make a clock. The heavy bombardment ended about 3.8 billion years ago, and impacts have been rarer ever since. So a heavily cratered surface is old, and a surface with few craters has been renewed by volcanism, tectonics, or erosion. Scientists estimate surface ages by .

Think of it this way

A heavily cratered surface is like a snow-covered parking lot that nobody has plowed: footprints (craters) pile up over time, so lots of footprints mean it hasn’t been cleared in a long while. A freshly plowed lot, like Earth’s young seafloor, has almost none.

5.4

Five geological histories

The Moon and Mercury are small and cooled early. Their surfaces are saturated with ancient craters. The Moon’s dark ("seas") are lava plains that flooded giant impact basins more than 3 billion years ago, the last gasp of its volcanism. Mercury’s long cliffs formed as the planet shrank while its huge iron core cooled.

Mars is intermediate in size. It had enormous volcanoes (Olympus Mons) and a planet-cracking tectonic canyon (Valles Marineris), but it has cooled and is nearly dead today. Its most intriguing features are dry riverbeds, lakebeds, and minerals that only form in water. Orbiters and rovers show that Mars once had flowing water, and possibly a thicker atmosphere, more than 3 billion years ago. Some water survives as ice in its polar caps and underground.

Venus is nearly Earth’s size and still geologically active, but it has no Earth-style plate tectonics. Its crater count suggests the whole surface was repaved by lava within the past billion years or so. Radar maps from orbiters, which see through its clouds, reveal thousands of volcanoes and strange tectonic features.

Earth is unique. Its lithosphere is broken into about a dozen plates that move a few centimeters a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. This builds mountains, opens oceans, and recycles crust, while liquid water erodes everything. Together they erase most craters and keep the surface young.

Part B

Planetary atmospheres

Blankets of gas that set a world’s climate, and how worlds gain and lose them.

5.5

What an atmosphere does

An is a layer of gas held by a world’s gravity. It creates pressure (at Earth’s surface, the weight of the air above you, about 1 bar), absorbs and scatters light, creates weather, and shields the surface from harmful radiation and small meteors. Most importantly for climate, it can make a world much warmer through the greenhouse effect.

Here is how the works. Visible sunlight passes through the air and warms the ground. The warm ground gives off infrared light. , mainly water vapor, carbon dioxide, and methane, absorb some of that infrared and re-emit it in all directions, including back down. The surface has to warm up until enough infrared finally escapes to balance the incoming sunlight.

Think of it this way

A greenhouse gas acts like a blanket on a cold night. The blanket doesn’t make heat; you do. It slows the rate at which your heat escapes, so you stay warmer. A thicker blanket (more greenhouse gas) means a warmer you.

Common misconception

“The greenhouse effect is bad.” The natural greenhouse effect is essential: without it, Earth’s average temperature would be about −18 °C and the oceans would freeze. The concern about climate change is that adding more greenhouse gases strengthens the effect beyond its natural level (Section 5.8).

5.6

Atmospheric structure, weather, and climate

Earth’s atmosphere has distinct layers. Nearly all weather happens in the lowest, the , which is warmed from below by the ground and churns with rising and sinking air. Above it, the contains the ozone layer, which absorbs dangerous ultraviolet sunlight. Higher still, the thin thermosphere and exosphere absorb X-rays and fade gradually into space.

is the day-to-day state of the atmosphere; is its long-term average. Global wind patterns come from two factors: the Sun heats the equator more than the poles, driving air circulation, and Earth’s rotation deflects moving air sideways (the ), breaking that circulation into bands. That is why the jet stream flows west to east and hurricanes spin.

Think of it this way

Try to roll a ball straight across a spinning merry-go-round: to someone riding it, the ball’s path curves. Air moving across the rotating Earth curves the same way, which is why storms spin counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere.

Other planets have weather too. Mars has planet-wide dust storms and seasonal carbon-dioxide frost. Venus’s upper atmosphere races around the planet in four days, though the planet takes 243 days to rotate once. Jupiter’s rapid rotation stretches its circulation into its striking bands (Section 5.9).

5.7

How atmospheres are gained and lost

Where did planetary atmospheres come from? The terrestrial worlds were too small and warm to capture gas from the solar nebula. Their atmospheres came mostly from , the release of gases trapped in their rocks by volcanoes. Volcanoes release mostly water vapor, carbon dioxide, and nitrogen. Additional gas came from impacts and, on cold worlds, from the evaporation or sublimation of surface ices.

Atmospheres are lost in several ways: condensation into surface ice, chemical reactions that lock gas into rocks, stripping by the solar wind (especially for worlds without a protective magnetic field), large impacts that blast gas away, and . In thermal escape, the fastest-moving gas molecules exceed the world’s and leak into space. Light molecules at high temperatures on small worlds escape most easily.

Think of it this way

Think of gas molecules as popcorn in a pot with a lid. Heat the pot more (higher temperature) or use lighter kernels (lighter gases), and more pop high enough to escape. A heavier lid (a bigger world with stronger gravity) keeps more in.

5.8

Three atmospheres, three fates

Venus, Earth, and Mars probably started with similar volcanic gases. Their very different fates show how size and distance from the Sun shape a world.

Mars: lost to space. Early Mars had a thicker atmosphere and flowing water. But as its small interior cooled, it lost its magnetic field and volcanic activity. Without a magnetic shield, the solar wind stripped away much of its air, NASA’s MAVEN orbiter has measured this loss directly, and the rest froze or was locked in rocks. Today its thin carbon-dioxide air has less than 1% of Earth’s surface pressure.

Venus: a runaway greenhouse. Closer to the Sun, Venus was too warm for its water to stay liquid; oceans, if it had any, evaporated. Water vapor, a greenhouse gas, trapped more heat, which evaporated more water: a . Ultraviolet sunlight broke the water molecules apart, and the hydrogen escaped to space. Without oceans, carbon dioxide could not dissolve and be locked into rocks, so it all stayed in the air, 90 times Earth’s surface pressure.

Earth: just right, with help. Earth kept liquid oceans, and oceans dissolve carbon dioxide and lock it into carbonate rocks such as limestone. The returns CO₂ to the air through volcanoes and keeps Earth’s climate within a livable range over millions of years, like a thermostat. Earth’s oxygen is a gift of life: photosynthesis has been adding it for more than 2 billion years.

Think of it this way

The three planets are like the three bowls of porridge in Goldilocks: Venus too hot, Mars too cold, Earth just right. But the story isn’t only about distance from the Sun. Size mattered for keeping heat and a magnetic field, and liquid water mattered for the carbon-cycle thermostat.

Human-caused climate change. By burning fossil fuels, humans have raised the carbon dioxide in Earth’s atmosphere from about 280 parts per million before the industrial era to more than 420 today, a level not seen in millions of years. The measurements and the physics are well established: more CO₂ strengthens the greenhouse effect, and Earth’s average temperature has risen about 1.2 °C since the late 1800s. Scientific assessments conclude that human activity is the dominant cause of the recent warming. Comparing Venus, Earth, and Mars is one reason scientists take the greenhouse effect seriously: it is the same physics on every world.

Part C

Jovian planet systems

Giant planets, their surprising moons, and their rings.

5.9

Inside the jovian planets

The jovian planets have no solid surface. Descend into Jupiter and the atmosphere just gets thicker, hotter, and denser. Beneath its clouds, hydrogen gas gradually becomes liquid under enormous pressure, and deeper still, it is squeezed into , a form that conducts electricity like a metal. At the center is a core of rock, metal, and hydrogen compounds, several times Earth’s mass.

Saturn’s interior is similar, with less metallic hydrogen. Uranus and Neptune have thinner hydrogen–helium envelopes around thick layers of hydrogen compounds (water, ammonia, methane), which is why they are called ice giants.

Jupiter and Saturn emit more heat than they receive from the Sun, still releasing energy from their formation and from slow internal settling. That heat, combined with rapid rotation (a Jupiter day is under 10 hours), drives powerful weather: bands of rising and sinking gas and huge storms. Jupiter’s , a high-pressure storm wider than Earth, has been observed for at least 190 years. Jupiter’s metallic hydrogen, churned by rapid rotation, also generates the strongest planetary magnetic field in the solar system.

Think of it this way

Jupiter is less like a ball of rock with air on top and more like a vast ocean of hydrogen with no seafloor you could ever reach: the deeper you go, the thicker and stranger the fluid gets.

5.10

A wealth of moons

The jovian planets have more than 400 known moons among them. Most are small, captured bodies, but several large moons are worlds as intriguing as any planet.

Jupiter’s four , discovered by Galileo in 1610, show a remarkable range:

  • Io is the most volcanically active world in the solar system, with hundreds of volcanoes and plumes of sulfur rising hundreds of kilometers high.
  • Europa has a young, cracked icy crust over a global ocean of liquid water, probably containing more water than all of Earth’s oceans.
  • Ganymede, the largest moon in the solar system (bigger than Mercury), likely has a subsurface ocean too, and generates its own magnetic field.
  • Callisto is heavily cratered and geologically quiet, though it may hide a deep ocean.

How can small, icy moons far from the Sun stay warm? The answer is . Io, Europa, and Ganymede are locked in an : for every orbit of Ganymede, Europa orbits twice and Io four times. The repeated gravitational tugs keep their orbits slightly stretched, so Jupiter’s strong tides squeeze and relax each moon as it moves closer and farther, and friction heats its interior.

Think of it this way

Bend a paper clip back and forth quickly and the bend gets warm. Io is flexed the same way, over and over, every 42 hours, and the heat keeps its interior molten.

Saturn’s moons are just as surprising. Titan, larger than Mercury, has a thick nitrogen atmosphere with a surface pressure 1.5 times Earth’s, and lakes and rivers of liquid methane and ethane at −180 °C. The Huygens probe landed there in 2005. Tiny Enceladus shoots geysers of water vapor and ice from cracks near its south pole, fed by an underground ocean; the Cassini spacecraft flew through them and found salt and organic molecules. Neptune’s moon Triton orbits backward, strong evidence that it is a captured Kuiper belt object.

5.11

Planetary rings

All four jovian planets have rings made of countless small particles, each orbiting the planet like a tiny moon. Saturn’s bright rings are mostly water ice; the others are darker, dustier, and fainter.

Rings form close to their planets for a reason. Inside a distance called the , the planet’s tidal forces are stronger than the gravity holding a moon together, so particles there cannot gather into a moon, and a moon that wanders too close is torn apart.

Think of it this way

Imagine trying to build a sandcastle on a shaking table: the closer you get to the motor, the harder it shakes, until nothing you build can hold together. Close to a giant planet, tidal forces are the shaking, and the sand stays scattered as a ring.

The rings are sculpted by moons. Gaps appear where ring particles would orbit in resonance with a moon (like the Cassini division, where particles would orbit exactly twice for each orbit of the moon Mimas), and small keep some narrow rings sharply confined.

Ring particles slowly collide, grind down, and spiral inward, so rings probably don’t last forever. Current thinking is that ring systems are continually renewed by debris from small moons that are chipped by impacts or torn apart. Saturn’s bright rings may be relatively young, perhaps a few hundred million years old; scientists are still debating the evidence.

5.12

What makes worlds different

Step back and a handful of factors explain most of what you’ve seen in this module:

  • Size controls how long a world keeps its internal heat, which powers volcanism, tectonics, magnetic fields, and outgassing, and how strongly its gravity holds an atmosphere.
  • Distance from the Sun sets the temperature, which decides whether water is ice, liquid, or vapor and how fast gases escape.
  • Rotation shapes weather patterns and helps generate magnetic fields.
  • Tidal heating, for moons, can substitute for size, keeping small worlds active.
Think of it this way

Think of each world as a recipe: the same basic ingredients, but the size of the pot, the heat of the stove, and how hard it is stirred decide what comes out. Earth got a big pot, a moderate stove, and steady stirring.

Pause and apply: a planet twice Earth’s size

Suppose a rocky planet twice Earth’s diameter orbited at Earth’s distance from its star. Would you expect it to be more or less geologically active than Earth? Better or worse at holding an atmosphere? (More active, and better at holding gases, since it keeps heat longer and has stronger gravity. Planets like this, called super-Earths, are common around other stars: Module 6.)

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • Terrestrial worlds have a metal core, rocky mantle, and thin crust, sorted by density through differentiation.
  • Internal heat drives geology. Larger worlds keep heat longer, so they stay volcanically and tectonically active; small worlds like the Moon and Mercury cooled early.
  • Four processes shape surfaces: impact cratering, volcanism, tectonics, and erosion. More craters mean an older surface.
  • Earth is the only world with plate tectonics; Venus was repaved by volcanism; Mars once had flowing water.
  • Greenhouse gases absorb infrared from the surface and keep a planet warmer; Earth’s natural greenhouse effect adds about 34 °C.
  • Atmospheres come from outgassing and are lost by escape, stripping, condensation, and chemical reactions. Light gases on small, hot worlds escape most easily.
  • Mars lost its air as it cooled and lost its magnetic field; Venus suffered a runaway greenhouse; Earth’s oceans and carbon cycle kept it livable. Rising CO₂ from human activity is strengthening Earth’s greenhouse effect.
  • Jovian planets have no solid surface; their hydrogen becomes liquid and metallic with depth. Internal heat and fast rotation drive banded weather and huge storms.
  • Tidal heating, sustained by orbital resonance, makes Io volcanic and keeps oceans liquid beneath icy crusts on Europa, Ganymede, and Enceladus.
  • Rings lie within the Roche limit, are shaped by moons, and are continually renewed by debris.

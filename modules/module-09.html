Module 9 of 13

The Sun and Measuring Stars

The Sun is the one star we can study up close, and it teaches us how every star works: a ball of gas balanced between gravity and the energy of fusion. Then we turn to the other stars. From nothing but their light, astronomers measure how far away they are, how bright and hot they really are, how much mass they contain, and even how old they are, and one diagram ties it all together.

Part A

Our star

How the Sun works, from its core to its storms.

9.1

Why the Sun shines

The Sun is a ball of hot plasma 109 times Earth’s diameter and 333,000 times its mass. Its own gravity pulls every part of it inward. What keeps it from collapsing? Pressure. The gas inside is hot and dense, and it pushes outward exactly hard enough to balance the inward pull. This balance is called .

Think of it this way

Picture a human pyramid of acrobats. The people at the bottom must push up hardest, because they support everyone above them. In the Sun, the gas deep inside supports the weight of all the layers above it, so it must be at the highest pressure, which means the highest density and temperature.

A second balance matters too: the Sun radiates 3.8 × 10²⁶ watts into space, so something must replace that energy. In the 1800s, scientists suggested burning (chemical reactions) or slow gravitational contraction. But chemical burning could keep the Sun going only a few thousand years, and contraction only about 25 million years. Rocks on Earth are billions of years old. Only releases enough energy to power the Sun for 10 billion years.

9.2

Fusion and the solar thermostat

In the Sun’s core, at 15 million K and 150 times the density of water, hydrogen nuclei (protons) fuse into helium through a series of steps called the . The net result:

4 hydrogen nuclei → 1 helium nucleus + energy + 2 neutrinos

The helium nucleus has about 0.7% less mass than the four protons that made it. That missing mass becomes energy, E = mc² (Module 7). The first step requires the weak force to turn a proton into a neutron, and protons must tunnel through their electrical repulsion (Module 8), which is why the Sun fuses slowly and steadily rather than exploding.

Fusion rates are extremely sensitive to temperature, which keeps the Sun stable. If the core heated up slightly, fusion would speed up, the extra energy would raise the pressure, the core would expand and cool, and fusion would slow back down. If the core cooled, the reverse would happen. This feedback is the .

Think of it this way

It works like the thermostat and furnace in a house, but in reverse: here the furnace itself expands the house when it runs too hot, which cools things down automatically. The Sun has held this balance for 4.6 billion years.

9.3

Journey through the Sun

Energy made in the core takes a long journey outward through distinct layers:

  • Core (inner quarter of the radius): fusion.
  • : energy moves as photons that are absorbed and re-emitted in random directions over and over. It takes something like 100,000 years or more for energy to random-walk through this zone.
  • (outer 30%): hot gas rises, cools, and sinks, carrying energy upward.
  • : the visible surface, at about 5,800 K, where light finally escapes. Its mottled look, , shows the tops of convection cells, each about the size of Texas.
  • Chromosphere and : the Sun’s atmosphere. Strangely, the corona is over a million degrees, far hotter than the surface, probably heated by magnetic energy.
Think of it this way

Energy escaping the radiation zone is like a person trying to leave a packed stadium by wandering randomly through the crowd: each step is short and in a random direction, so it takes an astonishingly long time to reach the exit. Once in the convection zone, it’s like catching an express escalator.

9.4

Solar activity and space weather

The Sun’s surface is anything but calm. Its churning, electrically conducting gas tangles its magnetic field, producing:

  • : darker, cooler regions (about 4,000 K) where strong magnetic fields block the flow of heat from below. They often come in pairs of opposite magnetic polarity.
  • Prominences: loops of hot gas suspended along magnetic field lines.
  • : sudden explosive releases of magnetic energy, emitting X-rays and fast particles.
  • : billions of tons of magnetized gas blasted into space.

Activity rises and falls in the , about 11 years long; the Sun’s overall magnetic field flips at each peak, so the full magnetic cycle is 22 years. The current cycle, Cycle 25, peaked around 2024–2025.

When a CME hits Earth, it can trigger a geomagnetic storm: brilliant auroras, disrupted radio and GPS signals, damaged satellites, and even power-grid failures. The 1859 Carrington Event sparked telegraph fires; in May 2024, a powerful storm painted auroras across skies as far south as Florida. Monitoring this space weather is now a practical job for NOAA’s Space Weather Prediction Center.

Think of it this way

Think of the Sun’s magnetic field lines as rubber bands being twisted by the Sun’s rotation and churning gas. Twist them enough and they snap, releasing their stored energy in a flare or flinging gas outward in a CME.

9.5

How we know what’s inside

We can’t see below the photosphere, yet we are confident about the Sun’s interior because of three independent lines of evidence:

  • Mathematical models built from the laws of physics predict the temperature and density at every depth, and match the Sun’s observed size, mass, and luminosity.
  • : the Sun vibrates with countless sound waves, detected as tiny Doppler shifts of its surface. Just as seismologists use earthquakes to map Earth’s interior, solar astronomers use these vibrations to measure conditions deep inside the Sun, confirming the models.
  • Solar neutrinos (Module 8) come straight from the core. For decades, detectors caught only a third to a half of the predicted number: the "solar neutrino problem." The solution, confirmed in 2001, was that neutrinos change type on their way to Earth. When all types are counted, the numbers match the fusion models.
Think of it this way

Helioseismology is like figuring out what is inside a wrapped present by tapping it and listening to how it rings. A bell, a book, and a box of rice all sound different, and so do different solar interiors.

9.6

Luminosity and apparent brightness

Two stars can look equally bright for very different reasons. is the total power a star emits, measured in watts or in units of the Sun’s luminosity. is how bright it looks from Earth. They are related by the inverse-square law (Module 3):

apparent brightness = luminosity ÷ (4π × distance²)
Think of it this way

A 100-watt bulb across a stadium can look dimmer than a 10-watt night-light beside your bed. To know which bulb is really more powerful, you need to know how far away each one is. The same is true of stars.

Astronomers also use the older , inherited from the ancient Greek astronomer Hipparchus, in which smaller numbers mean brighter stars: a magnitude 1 star is 100 times brighter than a magnitude 6 star (about the faintest visible to the eye). Apparent magnitude describes how bright a star looks; absolute magnitude describes how bright it would look from a standard distance of 10 parsecs.

9.7

Measuring distance with parallax

The most direct way to measure a star’s distance is (Module 1), the tiny apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant ones as Earth orbits the Sun. The parallax angle p is defined as half the total shift over six months. Astronomers define a unit of distance to make the math simple, the :

distance (parsecs) = 1 ÷ parallax angle (arcseconds)

One parsec is about 3.26 light-years. Even the nearest star, Proxima Centauri, has a parallax of only 0.77 arcsecond. The European Space Agency’s Gaia satellite has measured parallaxes for more than a billion stars with precision down to a few millionths of an arcsecond, creating a 3D map of much of our galaxy.

9.8

Temperature and spectral type

A star’s color reveals its surface temperature (Wien’s law, Module 3): red stars are cool, blue stars hot. Spectra give an even more precise measure, because the absorption lines present depend strongly on temperature. Astronomers sort stars into , from hottest to coolest:

O B A F G K M

Each type is divided into subtypes 0–9; the Sun is a G2 star. Students have long remembered the sequence with the mnemonic "Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me," or you can invent your own.

This scheme came from the Harvard College Observatory around 1900, where a team of women astronomers, known then as "computers," classified hundreds of thousands of stellar spectra. Annie Jump Cannon developed the OBAFGKM system and personally classified about 350,000 stars. In 1925 Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin showed that stars are made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium, a conclusion so surprising that she was urged to call it "spurious" until others confirmed it.

9.9

Measuring stellar masses

Mass is the most important property of a star, and the hardest to measure. The only direct method uses orbits: Newton’s version of Kepler’s third law (Module 3). Fortunately, about half of all stars are in systems, orbiting a companion:

  • Visual binaries: we can see both stars and watch them orbit.
  • Eclipsing binaries: the orbit is edge-on, so the stars pass in front of each other and the total brightness dips regularly.
  • Spectroscopic binaries: too close to see separately, but their spectral lines shift back and forth with the Doppler effect.

With the orbital period and separation, we can calculate the stars’ combined mass. In units of years, AU, and solar masses, the law is simple:

M₁ + M₂ = a³ ÷ p²
Think of it this way

You can’t weigh a star on a scale, but you can weigh it with a stopwatch and a measuring tape: time how long its companion takes to go around, measure how far apart they are, and gravity does the rest.

9.10

The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

Around 1910, Ejnar Hertzsprung and Henry Norris Russell independently plotted stars’ luminosities against their temperatures. The result, the , is the most important graph in stellar astronomy. By tradition, temperature increases to the left.

Stars don’t land just anywhere on it. About 90% fall along a diagonal band, the : stars fusing hydrogen in their cores, from hot, luminous blue stars at the upper left to cool, dim red dwarfs at the lower right. Above the main sequence lie and supergiants: cool but extremely luminous, which is only possible if they are enormous. Below lie : hot but dim, so they must be tiny.

Think of it this way

The H-R diagram is like a chart plotting people’s height against weight. Most people fall along a band, but outliers stand out, and each region tells you something about who is there. A star’s position tells you its size, stage of life, and more.

Why does position reveal size? A star’s luminosity depends on its surface temperature (each square meter emits in proportion to T⁴, Module 3) and on how many square meters it has. So a cool star that is very luminous must have an enormous surface: Betelgeuse is roughly 800 times the Sun’s radius, so large that it would swallow the orbit of Mars. Astronomers also label stars by : V for main sequence (the Sun is a G2 V star), III for giants, I for supergiants.

9.11

Mass rules the main sequence

Binary stars reveal a key fact: along the main sequence, mass determines everything. High-mass stars are at the hot, luminous upper left; low-mass stars at the cool, dim lower right. Main-sequence stars range from about 0.08 solar masses (below that, fusion never ignites) to roughly 150 solar masses.

Luminosity rises steeply with mass, roughly as mass to the 3.5 power. A star twice the Sun’s mass is about 11 times as luminous. That means massive stars burn through their fuel much faster, and their is far shorter:

lifetime ≈ 10 billion years × (mass ÷ Sun’s mass)⁻²·⁵
Think of it this way

A massive star is like a big-engine sports car with a larger fuel tank: it has more fuel, but it guzzles it so fast that it runs dry long before a small economy car. The smallest red dwarfs sip so slowly that they will shine for trillions of years.

9.12

Star clusters: measuring stellar ages

Stars are born in groups. , such as the Pleiades, contain a few thousand young stars loosely spread through the galactic disk. are dense, ball-shaped swarms of hundreds of thousands to millions of old stars orbiting in the galaxy’s halo. Because all the stars in a cluster formed together, at about the same time and distance, clusters are perfect laboratories for stellar lives.

On a cluster’s H-R diagram, the most massive stars have already used up their core hydrogen and left the main sequence. The point where the main sequence ends is the , and it reveals the cluster’s age: stars at the turnoff are just now reaching the end of their main-sequence lives.

Think of it this way

Imagine lighting a whole box of candles of different sizes at the same moment. Come back later and see which candles have burned out. If only the thinnest are still lit, a long time has passed. The turnoff is like the thickest candle still burning.

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • The Sun is in gravitational equilibrium: outward gas pressure balances gravity. Only nuclear fusion can power it for 10 billion years.
  • The proton–proton chain fuses 4 hydrogen nuclei into helium, converting 0.7% of the mass to energy; the solar thermostat keeps the rate steady.
  • Energy moves from the core through the radiation zone (slow random walk) and convection zone to the 5,800 K photosphere; the corona is over a million degrees.
  • Magnetic activity produces sunspots, flares, and CMEs on an 11-year cycle; CMEs cause auroras and space weather.
  • Helioseismology and solar neutrinos confirm models of the Sun’s interior.
  • Apparent brightness = luminosity ÷ 4πd². Distance in parsecs = 1 ÷ parallax in arcseconds; 1 pc ≈ 3.26 ly.
  • Spectral types OBAFGKM run from hottest to coolest; stars are mostly hydrogen and helium.
  • Binary star orbits give stellar masses: M₁ + M₂ = a³/p².
  • The H-R diagram shows the main sequence, giants, supergiants, and white dwarfs; position reveals radius.
  • Mass determines a main-sequence star’s luminosity and lifetime (≈ 10 billion years × M⁻²·⁵). Cluster turnoffs reveal ages; the oldest globular clusters are 12–13 billion years old.

The Building Blocks of the Universe

The biggest things in the universe depend on the smallest. Stars shine because protons tunnel through barriers they shouldn’t be able to cross. Dead stars hold themselves up because electrons refuse to share quantum states. Ghostly neutrinos stream out of the Sun and through your body by the trillions. This module introduces the quantum world and the particles and forces that build everything else.

Part A

The quantum world

Particles, forces, and the strange rules of the very small.

8.1

Inside the atom

In the early 1900s, physicists discovered that the rules governing atoms are nothing like the rules for baseballs and planets. The new rules became , developed by Max Planck, Einstein, Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg, Erwin Schrödinger, and others between 1900 and the late 1920s.

Every atom has a tiny, dense of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. The nucleus holds more than 99.9% of the atom’s mass, yet it is about 100,000 times smaller across than the atom itself.

Think of it this way

If a hydrogen atom were enlarged to the size of a football stadium, its nucleus would be a small pea on the 50-yard line, and the electron would be somewhere in the seats. Everything else is empty space. You, your chair, and the planet are made mostly of nothing.

Why don’t objects pass through each other if they are mostly empty? Because the electrons in their atoms repel one another electrically, and (as you will see) quantum rules keep electrons from being squeezed together.

8.2

Fundamental particles and antimatter

Protons and neutrons aren’t fundamental: each is made of three smaller particles called . A proton is two "up" quarks and one "down" quark; a neutron is one up and two down. Electrons, as far as we can tell, are truly fundamental. They belong to a family called , which also includes the ghostly (Section 8.10).

Physicists organize all known particles in the : six kinds of quarks, six kinds of leptons, and a set of force-carrying particles, such as the photon (which carries the electromagnetic force). The last piece, the Higgs boson, which is linked to how particles get mass, was discovered at CERN’s Large Hadron Collider in 2012.

Particles also divide into two broad classes. , such as quarks, electrons, protons, and neutrons, make up matter. , such as photons, carry forces. The difference matters enormously, as Section 8.6 will show.

Every particle has an partner with the same mass but opposite charge. The electron’s partner is the positron. When a particle meets its antiparticle, they , converting all their mass into energy (E = mc²). Antimatter isn’t science fiction: hospital PET scanners detect the light from positrons annihilating inside the body.

Think of it this way

Think of a particle and its antiparticle as a hole and the pile of dirt dug out of it. Put them together and both vanish, leaving flat ground, and in the particle case, a burst of pure energy.

8.3

The four fundamental forces

Every interaction in nature comes down to just four :

The four forces
ForceActs onRangeRelative strengthExamples
Strongquarks (protons, neutrons)size of a nucleus1holds nuclei together
Electromagneticcharged particlesunlimitedabout 1/137chemistry, light, electricity, friction
Weakquarks and leptonssmaller than a nucleusabout 10⁻⁶radioactive decay, first step of solar fusion
Gravityeverything with mass or energyunlimitedabout 10⁻³⁸orbits, stars, galaxies

If gravity is by far the weakest, why does it rule the cosmos? Because the strong and weak forces only reach across a nucleus, and electromagnetism has both attraction and repulsion: large objects have equal amounts of positive and negative charge, so their electrical forces cancel. Gravity only attracts, and it adds up. A planet’s worth of mass adds up to a lot of gravity.

Think of it this way

Imagine a tug-of-war where one team (electromagnetism) is incredibly strong, but on every rope half its members pull one way and half the other, so it never moves anything. A much weaker team (gravity) where everyone always pulls the same direction wins every large-scale contest.

8.4

Waves and particles

In Module 3 you saw that light behaves both as a wave and as a stream of photons. In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that matter does too: every moving particle has a wavelength, the :

λ = h ÷ (mass × speed)

where h is Planck’s constant, 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. Because h is so tiny, only very small particles have wavelengths big enough to notice.

The clearest demonstration is the . Fire electrons one at a time at a barrier with two narrow slits. Each electron lands at a single spot on the screen, like a particle. But as thousands build up, they form bright and dark stripes, an interference pattern that only waves passing through both slits can make. Stranger still, if you add detectors to see which slit each electron goes through, the pattern disappears.

8.5

The uncertainty principle

In 1927 Werner Heisenberg found a fundamental limit. The says that the more precisely we know a particle’s position, the less precisely we can know its momentum, and vice versa:

(uncertainty in position) × (uncertainty in momentum) ≥ h ÷ 4π

This is not a statement about clumsy instruments. It follows from the wave nature of matter: a wave spread out over a region doesn’t have a single location, and a wave squeezed into a small region must be built from many wavelengths, which means many momenta.

Think of it this way

Think about a musical note. A long, steady tone has a precise pitch but no precise moment in time. A very short click happens at a precise moment, but it has no clear pitch; it contains a smear of frequencies. You can’t have both at once. Position and momentum trade off in the same way.

8.6

The exclusion principle

In 1925 Wolfgang Pauli discovered a rule that shapes the entire material world. The says that no two fermions of the same type can occupy the same quantum state at the same time. Bosons have no such restriction: any number of photons can pile into the same state, which is how lasers work.

Think of it this way

Fermions behave like people in a theater with assigned seats: once a seat is taken, the next person must sit somewhere else, even if it means climbing to the balcony. Bosons are like a standing-room crowd that can pack into the same spot.

For atoms, this means electrons can’t all crowd into the lowest energy level. They fill up levels, or shells, from the inside out: 2 in the first shell, 8 in the next, and so on. The number of electrons in the outermost shell decides how an atom bonds with others, which is why elements in the same column of the periodic table behave alike. Without the exclusion principle, there would be no chemistry, and no life.

8.7

Degeneracy pressure: what holds up dead stars

Ordinary gas pressure comes from heat: hot particles move fast and push outward. Take away the heat and the pressure drops. So what keeps a dead star, one that has stopped generating energy, from being crushed by its own gravity?

The answer is quantum. When matter is squeezed to extreme density, its electrons are forced into a smaller space. The exclusion principle forbids them from sharing states, so they are forced into higher and higher energy states, and by the uncertainty principle, confined particles move fast. The result is , which doesn’t depend on temperature at all.

Think of it this way

Picture a game of musical chairs with far too few chairs, played in a room that keeps shrinking. As people are packed together, those who can’t find a seat are forced to keep moving faster and faster, pushing against the walls. Even if the room is freezing cold, that forced motion keeps pushing out.

  • supports white dwarfs, the Earth-sized remains of stars like the Sun, where a teaspoon of material would weigh several tons.
  • Neutron degeneracy pressure supports neutron stars, city-sized remains of massive stars, where a teaspoon would weigh about a billion tons.

Degeneracy pressure has limits. Above about 1.4 solar masses, the , electron degeneracy can’t hold up a white dwarf. You will see what happens then in Module 11.

8.8

Quantum tunneling: why the Sun shines

The Sun is powered by fusing hydrogen nuclei (protons). But protons are positively charged and repel each other strongly. At the Sun’s core temperature of 15 million K, classical physics says protons almost never get close enough to fuse; it would take temperatures of billions of degrees.

The answer is . Because a proton is also a wave, a small part of its wave extends through the barrier of electrical repulsion, giving it a small chance of appearing on the other side, close enough for the strong force to take over. The chance for any one collision is tiny, but the Sun’s core has so many protons colliding so often that fusion happens steadily, which is also why the Sun burns slowly enough to last billions of years.

Think of it this way

Imagine rolling a ball toward a hill it doesn’t have enough speed to climb. In everyday life it always rolls back. In the quantum world, every so often it simply appears on the other side of the hill, as if it had tunneled through. The thinner the hill, the better the odds.

8.9

Empty space isn’t empty

A related form of the uncertainty principle links energy and time: energy can be uncertain over very short times. In the quantum view, even perfectly empty space seethes with : pairs of particles and antiparticles that pop into existence and annihilate almost instantly. Their effects are small but measurable in the lab, where they slightly shift the energy levels of hydrogen and produce a tiny attraction between closely spaced metal plates.

Stephen Hawking realized in 1974 that near a black hole’s event horizon, one member of a virtual pair can fall in while the other escapes. The result is : black holes should very slowly give off energy and lose mass. For a black hole the mass of the Sun, the effect is so faint that evaporating would take about 10⁶⁷ years, far longer than the current age of the universe. It has not yet been observed, but it hints at how quantum physics and gravity might fit together.

Think of it this way

Think of the quantum vacuum as the surface of a calm-looking ocean seen up close: tiny ripples and bubbles constantly appear and vanish, even though the ocean as a whole looks perfectly still.

8.10

Neutrinos: the ghost particles

Neutrinos are produced in enormous numbers by fusion in the Sun: about 65 billion pass through every square centimeter of your body each second, day and night (at night they come up through Earth). They interact so weakly, feeling only the weak force and gravity, that almost all of them pass straight through Earth without stopping.

Think of it this way

For a neutrino, a light-year of solid lead would be about as transparent as a window pane is to sunlight. It is like trying to catch a single raindrop with a net whose holes are wider than a football field.

Detecting them takes huge detectors deep underground, shielded from other particles. Japan’s Super-Kamiokande is a tank of 50,000 tons of ultrapure water 1,000 m underground, lined with light sensors that catch the rare flash when a neutrino strikes an electron. In 1987, detectors caught about two dozen neutrinos from a supernova in a neighboring galaxy, confirming how massive stars die (Module 11). Measurements of solar neutrinos also revealed that neutrinos change type as they travel, proof that they have a tiny mass; that discovery earned the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physics.

8.11

Particles and the early universe

Particle physics and cosmology meet at the Big Bang. In the first fractions of a second, the universe was so hot and dense that it was a seething soup of quarks, leptons, photons, and antiparticles, constantly created and annihilating. Particle accelerators recreate tiny droplets of those conditions; CERN’s collisions of heavy nuclei briefly make a "quark-gluon plasma" like the one that filled the early universe.

One of the biggest puzzles is why there is any matter at all. The Big Bang should have made equal amounts of matter and antimatter, which would have annihilated completely, leaving only light. Instead, for roughly every billion antiparticles there were a billion and one particles. That tiny excess became every star, planet, and person. Physicists are still searching for the reason.

At the extreme energies of the earliest moments, the forces themselves may have been unified: the electromagnetic and weak forces are known to merge into a single "electroweak" force at high energies, and physicists suspect that the strong force, and perhaps even gravity, join them at still higher energies. You will return to this story in Module 13.

Think of it this way

Think of the early universe like water freezing. As liquid water cools, it suddenly organizes into ice with a different structure. As the universe cooled, the single unified force "froze out" into the separate forces we see today.

8.12

Quantum physics all around you

Quantum mechanics may sound abstract, but modern life runs on it. Transistors, the switches in every computer chip, rely on quantum behavior of electrons in semiconductors. LEDs and lasers emit light when electrons drop between quantum energy levels. The electronic detectors in astronomers’ cameras and in your phone (Module 3) count individual photons by the photoelectric effect, the discovery that won Einstein his Nobel Prize. MRI scanners, atomic clocks, and GPS all depend on quantum physics.

Big questions remain. What is dark matter, which appears to outweigh ordinary matter five to one (Module 13)? Might it be an undiscovered particle? And how can quantum mechanics and general relativity be combined into one theory of quantum gravity? Answering these may require discoveries as surprising as the quantum revolution itself.

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • Atoms are mostly empty space: a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons about 100,000 times farther out.
  • Protons and neutrons are made of quarks; electrons and neutrinos are leptons. The Standard Model organizes all known particles; the Higgs boson was found in 2012.
  • Every particle has an antiparticle; matter and antimatter annihilate into pure energy.
  • Four forces govern nature: strong, electromagnetic, weak, and gravity. Gravity is by far the weakest, but it dominates large scales because it only attracts and adds up.
  • Matter behaves as both particle and wave (de Broglie wavelength λ = h/mv), as the double-slit experiment shows. Waves confined in atoms explain quantized energy levels.
  • The uncertainty principle limits how precisely position and momentum can both be known; it comes from the wave nature of matter.
  • The exclusion principle forbids identical fermions from sharing a state, which structures atoms and chemistry.
  • Degeneracy pressure, from the exclusion and uncertainty principles, supports white dwarfs and neutron stars even when cold, up to limits such as 1.4 solar masses.
  • Quantum tunneling lets protons fuse in the Sun’s core at 15 million K; the Sun converts about 4 million tonnes of mass to energy each second.
  • Neutrinos stream from the Sun in vast numbers and barely interact; detecting them confirmed how the Sun shines and how massive stars explode.

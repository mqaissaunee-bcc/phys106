Module 11 of 13

Stellar Remnants and the Milky Way

Stars leave behind some of the strangest objects in the universe: Earth-sized white dwarfs where a teaspoon weighs tons, city-sized neutron stars spinning hundreds of times a second, and black holes. Then we zoom out to the Milky Way itself: a vast spiral of stars that recycles gas from one generation to the next, is held together mostly by invisible dark matter, and has a 4-million-solar-mass black hole at its heart.

Part A

The stellar graveyard

White dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes.

11.1

White dwarfs

A white dwarf is the exposed core of a low-mass star (Module 10): about the Sun’s mass squeezed into a ball the size of Earth. A teaspoon of its material would weigh several tons. It generates no energy; it simply glows with stored heat, cooling over billions of years. It is held up by electron degeneracy pressure (Module 8).

White dwarfs have a strange property: the more massive they are, the smaller they are. Adding mass strengthens gravity, which squeezes the electrons into a smaller space before degeneracy pressure can balance it. At about 1.4 solar masses, the , the electrons would need to move faster than light to hold it up, which is impossible, so no white dwarf can be heavier.

Think of it this way

Think of a white dwarf as a mattress made of electrons. Pile more weight on it and it compresses more, getting thinner and pushing back harder, but only up to a point. At 1.4 solar masses, the mattress gives out completely.

11.2

Novae and white dwarf supernovae

A white dwarf alone just fades away. But one in a close binary can be reborn. When its companion swells into a giant, gas spills over and spirals onto the white dwarf through an , heating up and glowing brightly.

Hydrogen piling up on the white dwarf’s surface eventually gets hot and dense enough to ignite fusion all at once, a thermonuclear flash that makes the system shine up to a million times brighter for a few weeks: a . The white dwarf survives, and the process can repeat.

If enough mass builds up to push the white dwarf past the Chandrasekhar limit (or two white dwarfs merge), carbon fusion ignites throughout it at once and the entire star is blown apart in a , leaving nothing behind. Because these explosions happen at nearly the same mass, they reach nearly the same peak luminosity, about 5 billion times the Sun’s. That makes them : measure how bright one looks, and you can calculate how far away it is. They led to the discovery of the accelerating universe (Module 13).

Think of it this way

A standard candle is like a 100-watt light bulb on a dark highway: if you know every bulb is 100 watts, you can judge how far away each one is just from how dim it looks.

Common misconception

“A nova is a new star.” The name comes from the Latin for "new," because a star appeared where none had been visible. In fact, it is an old white dwarf flaring up.

11.3

Neutron stars and pulsars

When a massive star’s core collapses (Module 10), protons and electrons merge into neutrons, and the core becomes a : typically 1.4–2 solar masses in a ball about 20 km across, held up by neutron degeneracy pressure. A teaspoon would weigh about a billion tons. Its surface gravity is so strong that escape speed is about half the speed of light.

Think of it this way

A neutron star packs more mass than the Sun into a sphere the size of a small city. Imagine the entire population of Earth crammed into a single sugar cube, and you get a sense of the density.

In 1967, graduate student Jocelyn Bell Burnell noticed a radio source pulsing every 1.337 seconds, so regular that it was jokingly nicknamed LGM-1, for "Little Green Men." It was the first : a rapidly spinning neutron star. Collapse shrank its rotating core so much that, by conservation of angular momentum, it spins many times per second; and it squeezed the core’s magnetic field to enormous strength. Beams of radiation stream out from its magnetic poles, and when a beam sweeps across Earth, we see a pulse, like a lighthouse.

11.4

Neutron stars in binaries and mergers

A neutron star in a close binary can pull gas from its companion. The gas spirals in through an accretion disk and heats to millions of degrees, making the system a bright . Infalling gas also transfers angular momentum, spinning the neutron star up to hundreds of rotations per second, which creates the millisecond pulsars.

Two neutron stars orbiting each other slowly spiral together as they radiate gravitational waves (Module 7). On August 17, 2017, LIGO and Virgo detected the gravitational waves from a merger, GW170817, 130 million light-years away. Two seconds later, a gamma-ray burst arrived, and telescopes around the world then observed a glowing whose spectrum showed freshly made heavy elements, including gold and platinum. It was the first cosmic event observed in both gravitational waves and light.

Think of it this way

GW170817 was like hearing an explosion before you see it. The gravitational waves were the sound of the two stars spiraling together; the light was the fireball. Getting both, from the same event, told astronomers far more than either alone.

11.5

Black holes: the end of the line

Neutron degeneracy pressure has a limit too, around 2–3 solar masses. If the collapsing core of a very massive star exceeds it, nothing can stop the collapse, and the core becomes a black hole (Module 7), with an event horizon of about 3 km per solar mass.

How do we find something that emits no light? Look for its effects. In X-ray binaries such as Cygnus X-1, a star orbits an invisible companion that pulls gas into a hot, X-ray-emitting accretion disk. Measuring the orbit gives the companion’s mass, about 21 solar masses for Cygnus X-1, far too massive to be a neutron star. Since 2015, gravitational-wave detectors have recorded hundreds of black hole mergers, revealing black holes from a few to over 100 solar masses.

The most powerful explosions known, , come in two kinds: long bursts from the collapse of extremely massive stars into black holes, and short bursts from neutron star mergers, like GW170817. For a few seconds, a burst can outshine a million galaxies.

Part B

Our galaxy

The Milky Way: its structure, recycling, rotation, and heart.

11.6

The Milky Way revealed

On a dark night, the Milky Way is a faint band of light across the sky: our edge-on view of the disk of the galaxy we live in. Galileo’s telescope showed it is made of countless stars. Its true shape took centuries to work out, because dust blocks our view and we see it from the inside.

Around 1918, Harlow Shapley noticed that globular clusters are not spread evenly around the sky but concentrated toward the constellation Sagittarius. He reasoned that they orbit the galaxy’s center, so the center must lie in that direction, far from the Sun. We now know the Milky Way has three main parts:

  • a thin about 100,000 light-years across, holding most of the stars, gas, and dust, arranged in spiral arms;
  • a central , a thick, bar-shaped concentration of older stars;
  • a roughly spherical of old stars and globular clusters, plus a much larger halo of dark matter.

The Sun lies in the disk about 26,000 light-years from the center, orbiting at about 230 km/s. One lap takes about 230 million years, a "galactic year."

Think of it this way

Mapping the Milky Way from inside is like trying to map a forest while standing in the middle of it on a foggy day: you can see the nearby trees, but you have to piece together the forest’s overall shape from clues, like the way the paths all seem to curve around one spot.

11.7

Galactic recycling

Stars and gas in the Milky Way are constantly recycled in the :

  1. Stars form in cold molecular clouds.
  2. They return gas to space through winds, planetary nebulae, and supernovae, enriched with the heavy elements they made.
  3. Supernovae blast out hot, expanding bubbles of gas, which eventually cool.
  4. The cooled gas collects into new molecular clouds, and the cycle repeats.

Each cycle adds heavy elements. The Sun formed from gas already enriched by many earlier generations, which is why Earth has the iron, silicon, and oxygen to be a rocky planet at all. Where young, hot stars shine on nearby gas, their ultraviolet light makes it glow in red, pink, and green as , such as the Orion Nebula.

Think of it this way

The star–gas–star cycle is like a city’s recycling program: old material is collected, processed, and made into new products, and each round adds a little more to the mix. The galaxy has been running this program for more than 12 billion years.

The cycle can’t run forever. Some gas is locked away for good in white dwarfs, neutron stars, black holes, and long-lived low-mass stars. Gradually, star formation will slow as the gas runs out.

11.8

Where stars form: spiral arms

The Milky Way’s spiral arms stand out because they are where stars are being born. Young, hot, blue stars, which live only a few million years, never travel far from where they formed, so they light up the arms. But the arms are not solid structures that rotate with the stars. They are : regions where orbiting stars and gas bunch up and move through slowly. As gas enters an arm, it is compressed, triggering star formation.

Think of it this way

Picture a traffic jam behind a slow truck on a highway. Cars slow down and bunch up as they reach the jam, then speed away past it. The jam moves slowly along the highway, but the cars in it are always changing. A spiral arm is a traffic jam of stars and gas, and the compressed gas "honks" into new stars.

11.9

Stellar populations and the galaxy’s history

Stars in the disk and in the halo are strikingly different. stars include young and old stars in nearly circular orbits, rich in heavy elements. stars are all old, move on elongated orbits at every angle, and contain very few heavy elements, sometimes less than 1/1,000 of the Sun’s amount.

These differences tell a story. The halo stars formed first, more than 12 billion years ago, from gas that had not yet been enriched, while the galaxy was still assembling. Gas later settled into a spinning disk (as in Module 4’s nebular theory), where star formation continues today. Astronomers now know the Milky Way grew partly by swallowing smaller galaxies. The Gaia satellite revealed streams of halo stars that are the remains of a dwarf galaxy nicknamed Gaia-Enceladus, which merged with the Milky Way about 10 billion years ago. The Milky Way is still consuming small galaxies today.

Think of it this way

Reading stellar populations is like reading layers in a city: old stone buildings in the historic district (the halo), and newer glass towers still going up downtown (the disk). The styles and materials reveal when each part was built.

11.10

The galaxy’s mass and dark matter

We can weigh the galaxy the same way we weigh the Sun: with an orbit. Using the Sun’s orbital speed and distance in Newton’s version of Kepler’s third law gives the mass inside the Sun’s orbit, about 100 billion solar masses. That is roughly the mass of the stars and gas we see.

But look farther out. A plots how fast stars and gas orbit at each distance from the center. If most mass were concentrated where the stars are, orbital speeds should drop in the outer galaxy, just as outer planets orbit the Sun more slowly. Instead, the Milky Way’s rotation curve stays nearly flat far beyond the visible disk. There must be much more mass out there than we can see: . It appears to make up roughly 90% of the galaxy’s total mass, in a vast halo. What dark matter is remains one of the biggest open questions in science (Module 13).

11.11

The heart of the galaxy

The center of the Milky Way is hidden from visible light by dust, but radio, infrared, and X-ray telescopes see through it. At the exact center sits a compact radio source called .

For about 30 years, two teams led by Andrea Ghez and Reinhard Genzel tracked individual stars orbiting it using infrared telescopes with adaptive optics (Module 3). One star, S2, completes an elongated orbit every 16 years, passing within about 120 AU of the center at nearly 3% of the speed of light. Kepler’s law gives the central mass: about 4 million solar masses, in a region smaller than our solar system. Only a supermassive black hole fits. Ghez and Genzel shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics, and in 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope imaged the glowing ring around Sagittarius A*’s shadow.

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • White dwarfs are Earth-sized remnants of low-mass stars; more massive ones are smaller, and none can exceed 1.4 solar masses.
  • Accreting white dwarfs can flare as novae or, past the Chandrasekhar limit, explode as white dwarf supernovae, which serve as standard candles.
  • Neutron stars are about 20 km across and as dense as atomic nuclei; spinning, magnetized ones are pulsars, which we see by the lighthouse effect.
  • Neutron stars in binaries form X-ray binaries and millisecond pulsars; the 2017 neutron star merger was seen in gravitational waves and light and made gold.
  • Cores above about 2–3 solar masses collapse into black holes, detected in X-ray binaries and gravitational-wave mergers.
  • The Milky Way has a disk about 100,000 light-years across, a central bulge, and a halo; the Sun orbits 26,000 light-years from the center every 230 million years.
  • The star–gas–star cycle recycles gas and enriches each generation with heavy elements; spiral arms are density waves where stars form.
  • Old, metal-poor halo stars formed first; the disk formed later, and the galaxy grew partly by swallowing smaller galaxies.
  • The flat rotation curve shows that dark matter makes up most of the galaxy’s mass.
  • Stars orbiting Sagittarius A* reveal a 4-million-solar-mass black hole at the galactic center.

Motion, Gravity, Light, and Telescopes

The two tools that unlock the universe. Gravity explains why everything moves the way it does, from falling apples to orbiting galaxies. Light is nearly all we ever receive from space, and learning to read it tells us what distant objects are made of, how hot they are, and how they move.

Part A

Motion, energy, and gravity

The rules that govern how everything in the universe moves.

3.1

Describing motion

Astronomy asks how things move: planets, stars, gas, galaxies. Physics gives a precise vocabulary for motion, and three words do most of the work.

is how fast something moves, such as 100 km/h. is speed together with direction: 100 km/h heading north. is any change in velocity: speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction.

Think of it this way

Drive around a traffic circle at a steady 30 km/h. Your speedometer never changes, yet you feel pushed toward the door. Your direction is changing, so your velocity is changing, and that is acceleration. A planet on a nearly circular orbit is always accelerating in exactly this way.

Near Earth’s surface, anything falling freely speeds up by about 9.8 m/s every second, whatever its mass. This , written g, means a dropped ball is falling at 9.8 m/s after one second, 19.6 m/s after two, and so on (ignoring air resistance).

Common misconception

“Heavy things fall faster.” Without air resistance, a hammer and a feather fall together. Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott tested this on the Moon in 1971 and they hit the ground at the same moment. On Earth, air slows the feather, not gravity.

Two more quantities matter. is mass times velocity: a loaded truck at 10 km/h is harder to stop than a bicycle at the same speed. A is anything that changes an object’s momentum. The net force is the combined effect of all the forces acting on something; if they cancel, the net force is zero.

3.2

Newton’s three laws of motion

In 1687 Isaac Newton published three laws that describe how forces change motion. They work equally well for shopping carts and galaxies.

First law: an object moves at constant velocity unless a net force acts on it. At rest stays at rest; moving keeps moving in a straight line. This tendency is called inertia.

Think of it this way

Pull a tablecloth out quickly and the dishes stay put; their inertia resists the sudden change. A space probe coasting between planets needs no engine to keep going, for the same reason: nothing is pushing back.

Second law: force equals mass times acceleration, F = ma. The same push gives a small mass a big acceleration and a large mass a small one.

Think of it this way

Push an empty shopping cart and it zips away; push a full one just as hard and it barely moves. Same force, different mass, different acceleration.

Third law: for every force there is an equal and opposite force. When you push on something, it pushes back on you just as hard.

Think of it this way

Two skaters stand face to face and push off each other. Both roll backward. A rocket works the same way: it pushes hot gas out the back, and the gas pushes the rocket forward. It needs nothing to push against, which is why rockets work in empty space.

Common misconception

“If Earth pulls on the Moon, the Moon doesn’t pull on Earth.” By the third law the two pulls are exactly equal. Earth barely responds because its mass is 81 times larger, but it does wobble slightly as the Moon orbits, which is part of how tides work.

3.3

Conservation laws

Some quantities can move from place to place or change form, but their total never changes. These conservation laws are among the most powerful tools in physics.

Conservation of momentum. The total momentum of interacting objects stays the same. The skaters in Section 3.2 start with zero total momentum and end with zero: equal amounts in opposite directions.

Conservation of . Angular momentum is the momentum of spinning or orbiting. Unless something twists it, it stays constant. For an orbit, it equals mass × speed × distance, so when a planet moves closer to the Sun it must speed up. That is the physics behind Kepler’s second law.

Think of it this way

A figure skater spins slowly with arms out, then pulls her arms in and spins much faster. She hasn’t pushed off anything; bringing mass closer to the spin axis simply requires a faster spin. Collapsing gas clouds spin up the same way as they shrink into stars and planetary systems (Module 4).

Conservation of energy. cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. Three basic forms cover almost everything in astronomy:

  • , the energy of motion (½mv²). The random motion of atoms and molecules is , and temperature measures its average per particle.
  • Radiative energy, carried by light.
  • , stored energy. Gravitational potential energy is released as things fall, and Einstein’s E = mc² says mass itself is a huge store of potential energy, the source of the Sun’s power.
Think of it this way

A cup of boiling coffee has a higher temperature than a warm bathtub, but the bathtub holds far more thermal energy, because it has vastly more water molecules. Temperature is how hot each particle is on average; thermal energy is the total. That is why the thin, million-degree gas in the Sun’s outer atmosphere wouldn’t cook you: there is almost nothing there.

3.4

The universal law of gravitation

Newton’s great insight was that the force pulling an apple to the ground is the same force holding the Moon in orbit. His says every mass attracts every other mass with a force

F = G × M₁ × M₂ ÷ d²

where M₁ and M₂ are the two masses, d is the distance between their centers, and G is a constant of nature. Two things follow. Doubling either mass doubles the force. And the force follows an : double the distance and the force drops to one-quarter; triple it and it drops to one-ninth.

Think of it this way

Picture a can of spray paint aimed at a wall. Stand twice as far back and the same paint spreads over a patch twice as wide and twice as tall, four times the area, so each square is a quarter as thick. Gravity (and, as you will see, light) thins out with distance in exactly this way.

Weighing the universe

Newton also showed where Kepler’s third law comes from, and in doing so turned orbits into scales. In his version,

p² = 4π² a³ ÷ [G (M₁ + M₂)]

If you can measure how long something takes to orbit (p) and how far it is from the body it orbits (a), you can calculate that body’s mass. This is how we know the masses of the Sun, planets with moons, stars in binary pairs, and even whole galaxies.

Orbits come in two families. Bound orbits are ellipses (circles included): the object returns again and again. Unbound orbits are open curves: the object passes once and leaves forever. To escape from Earth’s surface you need an of about 11 km/s (40,000 km/h). Moving between orbits requires a change in energy, from a rocket burn or a gravitational encounter with another body.

Weightless, but not gravity-free

Your weight is the force you feel pushing back from whatever supports you. In an elevator that suddenly accelerates downward you feel lighter; if the cable snapped, you and the elevator would fall together and you would float. Astronauts in orbit are in that same , continuously falling around Earth, so they feel weightless even though gravity at the Space Station is still about 90% of its strength on the ground.

Think of it this way

At the top of a big roller-coaster drop, your stomach floats and you rise off the seat for a moment. You and the car are falling together. An orbiting astronaut feels that moment continuously, for months.

Pause and predict: the Moon’s gravity on you

Does the Moon’s gravity pull on you right now? Yes, but it is so weak that the part that matters is its difference across Earth, which leads to tides.

Tides

The Moon pulls on every part of Earth, but more strongly on the side facing it and more weakly on the far side. This difference in pull stretches Earth, raising two of ocean: one toward the Moon and one directly away from it. As Earth rotates through both bulges, most coastlines get two high tides and two low tides a day.

The Sun raises tides too, about half as strong. When the Sun, Earth, and Moon line up at new and full moon, the two effects add up: extra-high (nothing to do with the season). At first and third quarter, the Sun and Moon pull at right angles and partly cancel: smaller neap tides.

Tides also slowly change orbits. Friction as Earth rotates beneath the bulges gradually slows Earth’s spin, lengthening the day by about 2 milliseconds per century, while the Moon drifts about 3.8 cm farther away each year. The same tidal forces long ago locked the Moon into synchronous rotation (Module 1).

3.6

What light is

Almost everything we know about the universe beyond our solar system arrives as light. So what is it? Light behaves in two ways, depending on how you look.

Light as a wave. Light is an : a traveling ripple of electric and magnetic fields. Like any wave it has a (the distance between crests) and a (how many crests pass each second). In a vacuum all light travels at the same speed, c = 300,000 km/s, so wavelength and frequency are tied together: wavelength × frequency = speed of light (λ × f = c) Longer waves have lower frequencies.

Think of it this way

Stand on a pier and count ocean waves passing a post. Long, lazy swells pass slowly (low frequency); short, choppy waves pass quickly (high frequency). Since all light moves at the same speed, a longer wavelength always means fewer waves per second.

Light as a particle. Light also arrives in tiny packets of energy called . A photon’s energy is set by its frequency: E = hf, where h is Planck’s constant. Higher frequency (shorter wavelength) means more energetic photons. That is why ultraviolet light can sunburn you and X-rays can pass through tissue, while radio waves pass through you harmlessly.

The electromagnetic spectrum

Visible light, from violet at about 400 nm to red at about 700 nm, is a tiny slice of the full . Beyond red lie infrared, microwaves, and radio waves; beyond violet lie ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays. All of them are the same phenomenon at different wavelengths, and astronomers now observe the universe across all of them.

3.7

Matter, atoms, and light

To understand where light comes from, we need atoms. Every atom has a tiny nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. The number of protons (the ) decides which element an atom is: 1 for hydrogen, 2 for helium, 6 for carbon. Versions of an element with different numbers of neutrons are .

Electrons in an atom cannot have just any energy. They occupy specific . An electron can jump up a level by absorbing a photon with exactly the right energy, or drop down a level by emitting a photon with exactly the energy difference.

Think of it this way

Energy levels are like the rungs of a ladder: you can stand on a rung, but not between rungs. Moving up takes a specific amount of energy, and stepping down releases that same amount. Because each element’s ladder has its own unique rung spacing, each element emits and absorbs its own unique set of colors.

This is the key idea behind reading the universe: every element has a unique pattern of wavelengths, a spectral fingerprint. Hydrogen, for example, emits a strong red line at 656.3 nm whenever its electrons drop from the third level to the second.

3.8

Three kinds of spectra

Spread light into its colors with a prism or grating and you get a . There are three basic kinds, and which one you see tells you about the source:

  1. A , a smooth rainbow with no gaps, comes from a hot, dense object such as a light-bulb filament or the dense interior of a star.
  2. An , bright lines on a dark background, comes from a hot, thin gas such as a neon sign or a glowing nebula.
  3. An , a rainbow with dark lines, comes from cooler, thin gas in front of a hot source. Stars show absorption spectra, because their cooler outer layers absorb light from the hotter layers below.
Think of it this way

Think of a spectrum as a barcode. The grocery scanner reads the pattern of lines to identify a product; astronomers read the pattern of lines to identify elements. Absorption and emission are the same barcode printed in negative: the wavelengths a gas absorbs are exactly the ones it emits.

3.9

Thermal radiation: temperature and color

Every object with a temperature above absolute zero glows with , a continuous spectrum whose shape depends only on temperature. Stars are close to ideal thermal emitters, and so, in infrared light, are you. Two laws describe this glow.

1. Hotter objects emit more light per square meter at every wavelength. The total rises steeply, as temperature to the fourth power (): double the temperature and each square meter emits 16 times as much.

2. Hotter objects emit their peak light at shorter wavelengths. The peak wavelength is inversely proportional to temperature (): peak wavelength (nm) ≈ 2,900,000 ÷ T (kelvin) The Sun, at 5,800 K, peaks near 500 nm, in visible light.

Think of it this way

Turn on an electric stove burner. At first it gives off heat you can feel but not see (infrared). As it heats, it glows dull red, then orange. If it could get hotter still, it would turn yellow-white and then blue-white. Stars follow the same sequence: red stars are the coolest, blue stars the hottest.

3.10

The Doppler effect

Motion changes the wavelength of light. If a source moves away from you, its waves get stretched to longer wavelengths, a . If it moves toward you, they get squeezed to shorter wavelengths, a . This is the .

Think of it this way

Listen to an ambulance siren: the pitch is higher as it approaches and drops as it passes and races away. The sound waves are bunched up ahead of the ambulance and stretched out behind it. Light does exactly the same thing, but because light is so fast, the shifts are tiny unless the source is moving very fast.

For speeds much less than light, the shift gives the speed directly:

speed = (shift in wavelength ÷ rest wavelength) × c

Because every line in a spectrum shifts by the same fraction, astronomers compare an object’s lines with the known laboratory wavelengths (the fingerprints of Section 3.8) to measure how fast it moves toward or away from us.

Common misconception

“A redshifted star looks red.” No: for ordinary stars the shift is a tiny fraction of a nanometer, far too small to change the color you see. Astronomers detect it by precisely measuring the positions of spectral lines.

The Doppler effect only measures motion along our line of sight (toward or away). Sideways motion does not shift lines. It also reveals rotation: when a star or planet spins, one side approaches while the other recedes, which broadens its spectral lines. And tiny periodic Doppler shifts in a star’s light revealed many of the first known planets around other stars (Module 6).

3.11

How telescopes work

A telescope does two main jobs, and both depend on its size.

Collect light. A telescope’s depends on the area of its main mirror or lens, which grows as the square of its diameter, the . A 2.4-meter telescope has about 100,000 times the collecting area of your 7-millimeter pupil, so it can see objects 100,000 times fainter.

Think of it this way

Collecting starlight is like catching rain in a bucket. A bucket twice as wide catches four times as much rain in the same time. Astronomers want the biggest buckets they can build.

See fine detail. is the smallest angle between two objects that the telescope can still see as separate. Waves spread out slightly as they pass through any opening (diffraction), which sets a limit:

resolution (arcseconds) ≈ 250,000 × wavelength ÷ aperture

Bigger apertures and shorter wavelengths give sharper images. Your eye can split objects about 1 arcminute apart; the Hubble Space Telescope resolves about 0.05 arcsecond, like reading a newspaper headline from 4 km away.

Refractors and reflectors

A refracting telescope uses a lens to bend light to a focus. Galileo’s telescopes were refractors. Large lenses sag under their own weight and bend colors differently, so no refractor has been built larger than about 1 meter. A reflecting telescope uses a curved mirror instead. Mirrors can be supported from behind and built in segments, which is why every large research telescope today is a reflector.

3.12

Telescopes and the atmosphere

Earth’s atmosphere is a mixed blessing for astronomers. It causes three problems:

  • . Scattered city light brightens the sky and drowns out faint objects, which is why observatories are built on remote mountaintops.
  • Turbulence. Moving pockets of air bend starlight back and forth, making stars twinkle and blurring images. Astronomers measure this as . High, dry sites with smooth airflow, like Mauna Kea in Hawai‘i or the Atacama Desert in Chile, have the best seeing.
  • Absorption. The atmosphere blocks most of the spectrum. Visible light, radio waves, and a few narrow slices of infrared get through; ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays, and most infrared do not. That is good for life on the surface and bad for astronomers.
Think of it this way

Looking at stars through the atmosphere is like looking at a coin at the bottom of a swimming pool while people splash in the water: the coin seems to shimmer and wobble. Stars twinkle for the same reason. Planets, which appear as tiny disks rather than points, twinkle much less.

There are two solutions. Ground-based telescopes use : a flexible mirror that changes shape hundreds of times a second to undo the atmosphere’s blurring. And for wavelengths the air blocks, telescopes go to space: the Chandra X-ray Observatory, the Hubble Space Telescope (ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared), and the James Webb Space Telescope (infrared), which orbits 1.5 million km from Earth where it can stay cold enough to detect faint infrared glow.

Radio telescopes face a different problem: radio waves are so long that even a 100-meter dish has poor resolution. The answer is , linking many dishes so they act like one giant telescope as wide as the distance between them. The Event Horizon Telescope linked dishes across Earth to capture the first images of black holes’ shadows (Module 11).

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • Velocity is speed plus direction, and acceleration is any change in velocity, including a change of direction. Near Earth, falling objects accelerate at 9.8 m/s² regardless of mass.
  • Newton’s laws: motion continues unless a net force acts; F = ma; every force has an equal and opposite partner.
  • Momentum, angular momentum, and energy are conserved. Angular momentum explains why orbiting bodies speed up when closer and collapsing clouds spin faster.
  • Temperature is the average energy per particle; thermal energy is the total. Mass is a form of energy (E = mc²).
  • Gravity follows an inverse-square law. Newton’s version of Kepler’s third law lets us measure masses from orbits.
  • An orbit is continuous free fall. Bound orbits are ellipses; escaping Earth takes about 11 km/s. Orbiting astronauts float because they fall with their spacecraft.
  • Tides come from the difference in the Moon’s (and Sun’s) pull across Earth. Spring tides occur at new and full moon; neap tides at the quarters.
  • Light is both a wave and a stream of photons. Shorter wavelength means higher frequency and more energetic photons.
  • Each element has unique energy levels and therefore a unique spectral fingerprint. Hot dense objects give continuous spectra, hot thin gas gives emission lines, and cool gas in front of a hot source gives absorption lines.
  • Hotter objects emit more light per area (∝ T⁴) and peak at shorter wavelengths (Wien’s law), so star color reveals temperature.
  • The Doppler shift of spectral lines measures motion toward or away from us.
  • A telescope’s aperture sets its light-collecting area and its angular resolution. Space telescopes avoid atmospheric absorption and blurring; adaptive optics and interferometry push ground telescopes further.

The Big Bang and the Fate of the Universe

Run the expansion of the universe backward and everything converges into a state of unimaginable heat and density 13.8 billion years ago. This module tells the story of the universe’s first moments, the evidence that the story is true, and the two great mysteries that dominate the cosmos today: dark matter and dark energy. It ends where the universe might end, and with a review and practice final for the second half of the course.

Part A

The Big Bang

The universe’s first moments and the evidence they left behind.

13.1

The hot early universe

If the universe is expanding (Module 12), it must have been smaller, denser, and hotter in the past. Compress a gas and it heats up; the same is true of the whole universe. Run the clock back 13.8 billion years and you reach the : not an explosion in space, but the rapid expansion of space itself from an extremely hot, dense state.

Common misconception

“The Big Bang was an explosion at a point in space.” There was no center and no outside. Every place in the universe was part of the hot, dense early state, and space has been expanding everywhere at once ever since.

As the universe expanded, it cooled, and different things could happen at different temperatures. Physicists divide the early universe into eras:

  • Planck era (before 10⁻⁴³ s): current physics cannot describe it; a theory of quantum gravity is needed.
  • GUT era (to 10⁻³⁸ s): the strong, weak, and electromagnetic forces were unified.
  • Electroweak era (to 10⁻¹⁰ s): the weak and electromagnetic forces were still one; inflation happened at the start.
  • Particle era (to 0.001 s): particles and antiparticles constantly formed and annihilated.
  • Era of nucleosynthesis (to 3 minutes): protons and neutrons fused into helium.
  • Era of nuclei (to 380,000 years): a glowing plasma of nuclei and electrons.
  • Era of atoms (to about 1 billion years): atoms formed, the universe became transparent, and the first stars ignited.
  • Era of galaxies (to today): galaxies grew into the forms we see now.

13.2

Matter from energy

In the particle era, the universe was so hot that photons carried enough energy to create particles: a pair of photons could turn into a particle and its antiparticle (E = mc² in reverse), and pairs annihilated back into photons just as quickly. As the universe cooled, photons no longer had enough energy to make new pairs, and nearly all the matter and antimatter annihilated.

Nearly, but not quite. For roughly every billion antiquarks there were a billion and one quarks (Module 8). The leftovers became all the protons and neutrons in the universe. That is why, today, there are about a billion photons for every proton, and why there is anything at all rather than just light.

Think of it this way

It’s like a dance where partners pair off and leave the floor together. If there had been exactly equal numbers of dancers of each kind, the floor would end up empty. One extra dancer in every billion is all that remains, and that remnant is everything we see.

13.3

The first elements

From about 0.001 s to 3 minutes, the whole universe was as hot as a star’s core. Protons and neutrons fused, a process called . Neutrons are a bit heavier than protons and less were made; by the time fusion began, there were about 7 protons for every neutron. Almost every neutron ended up in helium-4 (2 protons + 2 neutrons). After 3 minutes, the universe had cooled too much for further fusion.

The prediction: ordinary matter should be about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass, with traces of deuterium and lithium and essentially nothing heavier. That is exactly what we find in the oldest stars and least-processed gas clouds, a striking confirmation. The small amount of deuterium also tells us the density of ordinary matter: only about 5% of the critical density (Section 13.10).

13.4

The cosmic microwave background

For 380,000 years the universe was a glowing plasma, and photons couldn’t travel far before bouncing off free electrons, like light in a fog. When the universe cooled to about 3,000 K, electrons joined nuclei to form neutral atoms (recombination). Suddenly the fog cleared and light could stream freely. That light has been traveling ever since, and as space expanded about 1,100 times, its wavelengths stretched from near-infrared into microwaves. Today it is the (CMB), a faint glow at 2.7 K coming from every direction.

Think of it this way

Imagine being in a thick fog that suddenly lifts. The last glow reflected off the fog before it cleared is what you see as the edge of your view. The CMB is the glow from the moment the universe’s fog lifted, and it surrounds us on all sides because the fog was everywhere.

Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson discovered the CMB accidentally in 1965, as persistent noise in a radio antenna at Bell Labs in Holmdel, New Jersey, just a few miles from Brookdale. It earned them the Nobel Prize. The COBE satellite later showed its spectrum is a nearly perfect thermal (blackbody) spectrum, exactly as the Big Bang predicts. The WMAP and Planck satellites mapped tiny temperature differences, about 1 part in 100,000, which are the seeds of every galaxy.

13.5

Inflation

The Big Bang theory left some puzzles. Why is the CMB almost exactly the same temperature in opposite directions, when those regions were too far apart to ever exchange heat? Why is the universe’s geometry so close to perfectly flat? And where did the tiny ripples that grew into galaxies come from?

The leading answer is : during the first tiny fraction of a second, perhaps around 10⁻³⁶ s, the universe expanded enormously, by a factor of perhaps 10²⁵ or more, in far less than a trillionth of a second. Regions that had been in contact and reached the same temperature were flung far apart; any curvature was stretched flat; and microscopic quantum fluctuations were stretched to cosmic size, becoming the seeds of galaxies.

Think of it this way

Inflate a wrinkled balloon to the size of Earth: any small patch would look perfectly smooth and flat, as Earth’s surface looks flat to us. And a tiny mark on the original balloon would be stretched into a huge pattern, just as quantum ripples became the structures of the cosmic web.

Inflation’s predictions, such as the pattern of the CMB’s ripples, match observations well, but it remains an active area of research; physicists do not yet know what caused it.

13.6

The evidence for the Big Bang

The Big Bang is not a guess. It is a scientific theory (Module 2) supported by several independent lines of evidence:

  • Expansion: Hubble’s law shows galaxies moving apart (Module 12).
  • The CMB: a nearly perfect thermal glow at exactly the predicted temperature, filling the sky.
  • Light-element abundances: 75% hydrogen, 25% helium, and trace deuterium and lithium, as predicted.
  • Galaxy evolution: distant galaxies, seen as they were long ago, look younger and different from nearby ones (Module 12).
  • Age agreement: the oldest stars (12–13 billion years) are younger than the 13.8-billion-year-old universe, as they must be.

Even the dark night sky is a clue. In an infinite, unchanging universe filled with stars, every line of sight would eventually end on a star, and the whole sky would blaze as bright as the Sun. This puzzle is called . The solution: the universe has a finite age, so we can see only the light that has had time to reach us, and expansion redshifts the most distant light out of view.

Think of it this way

Stand in a large forest and every direction ends on a tree trunk. If the forest went on forever, you’d see only bark in every direction. A dark night sky is like seeing gaps between trees: evidence that the forest of stars isn’t infinite and eternal.

Dark matter, dark energy, and destiny

What the universe is made of and how it may end.

13.7

Dark matter

You have met the evidence for in pieces: the flat rotation curves of galaxies (Module 11), fast-moving galaxies and hot gas in clusters, and gravitational lensing (Module 12). The Bullet Cluster, two clusters that collided, is especially telling: lensing shows most of the mass passed through the collision unaffected, separated from the hot gas that slowed down. Only invisible, weakly interacting matter behaves that way. The CMB’s pattern of ripples independently requires it too.

Together, the evidence says dark matter is about 27% of the universe’s total content, more than five times as much as ordinary matter. It can’t be ordinary matter hiding in dim stars or gas, because Big Bang nucleosynthesis limits ordinary matter to about 5%. Most physicists think it is an undiscovered kind of particle, such as WIMPs (weakly interacting massive particles) or axions. Deep underground detectors and particle colliders are searching; none has found it yet. A minority of scientists explore whether gravity itself works differently on large scales.

Think of it this way

Imagine seeing a friend’s dog-walking leash tugging and swerving in the dark, though you can’t see the dog. You can tell something with mass is there, and even roughly how big it is, from the tugs alone. Astronomers “see” dark matter only through its gravitational tugs.

13.8

Building the cosmic web

Dark matter was essential for building galaxies. The ripples from inflation, seen in the CMB, were too faint for ordinary matter alone to grow into galaxies in the time available, because ordinary matter was held back by radiation until atoms formed. Dark matter, which doesn’t interact with light, started clumping earlier. By the time atoms formed, dark matter had already built a scaffold of dense regions, and ordinary gas fell into them to form stars and galaxies (Module 12).

Supercomputer simulations that start with the ripples measured in the CMB and include dark matter produce a cosmic web of filaments, clusters, and voids that looks remarkably like the real distribution of galaxies. Without dark matter, the simulations fail.

Think of it this way

Dark matter is like the invisible steel frame of a building going up. Ordinary matter is the visible glass and brick attached to it. You notice the finished facade, but the frame came first and determined the shape.

13.9

Dark energy and the accelerating universe

Gravity pulls, so astronomers expected the expansion of the universe to be slowing down. In 1998, two teams measured white dwarf supernovae (Module 11) in distant galaxies to find out how much. The supernovae were fainter, and so farther away, than expected: the expansion is not slowing but speeding up. The discovery earned the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Whatever drives the acceleration is called . It makes up about 68% of the universe’s total content, spread evenly through space. The simplest explanation is a "cosmological constant," an energy of empty space itself, an idea Einstein once introduced and later abandoned. Recent large surveys, such as DESI, have hinted that dark energy might change over time; if confirmed, that would be a major discovery, but the question is not yet settled.

Think of it this way

Throw a ball upward and you expect it to slow down. Imagine instead watching it slow down for a while, and then start speeding up, rising faster and faster. That is roughly what astronomers found the universe doing, and "dark energy" is the name for whatever is pushing it.

Putting it together, the universe is about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter, and 68% dark energy. Everything we have studied in this course, every planet, star, and galaxy, is part of that 5%.

13.10

The fate of the universe

Whether the universe expands forever depends on a tug-of-war between matter’s gravity and dark energy. Cosmologists compare the universe’s density with the , the density at which space would be geometrically flat, only about 5 or 6 hydrogen atoms per cubic meter. Without dark energy, a universe denser than critical would eventually stop expanding and recollapse in a "Big Crunch," and a less dense one would expand forever.

Measurements of the CMB show that our universe is flat to within less than 1%: matter plus dark energy add up to the critical density. But because dark energy dominates and is causing acceleration, the universe is expected to expand forever. Galaxies beyond our Local Group will eventually recede out of sight; stars will burn out over trillions of years; and the universe will grow ever colder and darker, a slow "Big Freeze." A more dramatic "Big Rip" would require dark energy to grow stronger over time, which current evidence does not favor.

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • The universe began 13.8 billion years ago in a hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since; the Big Bang happened everywhere, with no center.
  • The early universe passed through distinct eras as it cooled, from the Planck era through the eras of nucleosynthesis, nuclei, atoms, and galaxies.
  • Matter formed from energy; a one-in-a-billion excess of matter over antimatter survived to become everything we see.
  • Big Bang nucleosynthesis made the universe about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass, as observed.
  • The CMB is light released when atoms formed at 380,000 years, now cooled to 2.7 K; its tiny ripples seeded galaxies.
  • Inflation, a brief period of enormous expansion, explains why the universe is so smooth and flat and where structure came from.
  • Expansion, the CMB, light-element abundances, galaxy evolution, and even the dark night sky support the Big Bang.
  • Dark matter (about 27%) outweighs ordinary matter (about 5%) more than five to one and built the scaffold for galaxies.
  • Distant supernovae revealed that the expansion is accelerating, driven by dark energy (about 68%).
  • The universe is flat and, dominated by dark energy, is expected to expand forever, growing colder and darker.

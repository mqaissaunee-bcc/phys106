Part A

The cosmic perspective

Where we are, how big the universe is, and how long it has existed.

1.1

Our cosmic address

If you mailed a letter to someone on another planet in another galaxy, how would you write your return address? Your street, town, state, and country get you as far as Earth. Astronomy supplies the rest of the lines, and each line describes a structure far larger than the one before it.

Think of it this way

Your cosmic address works like a set of nesting dolls. Open the universe and you find superclusters; open one of those and you find groups of galaxies; open ours and you find the Milky Way, then the solar system, then Earth. Each doll is not just a little bigger than the one inside it. It is thousands to millions of times bigger.

We live on Earth, a rocky orbiting a we call the Sun. The Sun and everything bound to it by gravity make up our : eight planets, well over two hundred known moons, several dwarf planets such as Pluto, and enormous numbers of asteroids and comets.

The Sun is one of a few hundred billion stars in the , a flattened spiral . We sit in the disk, a bit more than halfway from the center to the visible edge. On a dark night, away from city lights, the faint band of light stretching across the sky is our edge-on view into that disk. Every individual star you can see with your unaided eye belongs to the Milky Way.

The Milky Way is one of the two largest members of the , a collection of several dozen galaxies. The other large member is the Andromeda Galaxy, about 2.5 million light-years away. The Local Group in turn sits on the outskirts of a , a sprawling network of galaxy groups and clusters. Superclusters are the largest structures we know of, and together they make up the .

Two words cause confusion early on, so let us pin them down. A is anything that orbits a planet, not only Earth's Moon. And a galaxy is not the same as the solar system: a galaxy contains billions of stars, most of which probably have planets of their own.

1.2

Measuring the immense

The numbers in astronomy are so large that ordinary units stop being useful. Measuring the distance to Andromeda in kilometers is like measuring the distance from New York to Los Angeles in grains of sand. Astronomers handle this in two ways: a compact way of writing big numbers, and bigger units.

Powers of ten

Writing out 150,000,000 kilometers is clumsy, and counting zeros invites mistakes. writes the same number as 1.5 × 10⁸ km: a number between 1 and 10, times ten raised to a power. The power tells you how many places to move the decimal point. Positive powers make numbers bigger (10³ = 1,000); negative powers make them smaller (10⁻³ = 0.001).

Each step up in power is a factor of ten, which means the steps add up faster than intuition expects. That is the whole trick of astronomy's scales.

Think of it this way

"Million" and "billion" sound like neighbors, but they are not. A million seconds is about 11½ days. A billion seconds is about 32 years. A trillion seconds is about 32,000 years, longer than all of recorded history. Every time astronomy jumps a power of ten, picture that jump from days to decades.

Worked example: multiplying in scientific notation

Light travels 3.0 × 10⁵ km every second, and a year has about 3.16 × 10⁷ seconds. How far does light travel in a year?

(3.0 × 10⁵) × (3.16 × 10⁷) = (3.0 × 3.16) × 10⁽⁵⁺⁷⁾ ≈ 9.5 × 10¹² km

Multiply the front numbers, add the powers. That answer is a light-year, which is next.

Bigger units

Inside the solar system we use the (AU), Earth's average distance from the Sun: about 150 million kilometers. Jupiter orbits at about 5 AU; Neptune at about 30 AU.

Beyond the solar system we use the . Light travels about 300,000 kilometers every second, fast enough to circle Earth seven and a half times in that second. In a full year it covers about 9.46 trillion kilometers, and that distance is one light-year.

Think of it this way

You already measure distance with time. "The mall is 20 minutes away" tells someone a distance, using how long it takes to get there at highway speed. A light-year is the same idea with the fastest speed there is: the distance light covers in a year of travel.

Common mix-up

A light-year is a distance, not a length of time. "The star is 10 light-years away" tells you where it is. It also tells you that its light took 10 years to reach you, which is where the confusion comes from.

Looking far away means looking back in time

Because light takes time to travel, you never see anything as it is right now. You see the Moon as it was about 1.3 seconds ago and the Sun as it was about 8⅓ minutes ago. When you look at a star 500 light-years away, you see light that left it 500 years ago. That delay is called , and it is one of the most useful facts in astronomy: by looking at objects at different distances, we see the universe at different ages.

Think of it this way

Imagine a pen pal who lives so far away that letters take ten years to arrive. Every letter you open describes their life a decade ago. Starlight is a letter like that. A thunderstorm shows the same effect on a small scale: you see the lightning first and hear the thunder later, because sound is slower than light.

There is a limit. The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, so we cannot receive light that has been traveling longer than that. The region from which light has had time to reach us is the . Whatever lies beyond it is not necessarily empty; its light simply has not arrived yet.

Worked example: converting light-years

Proxima Centauri, the nearest star beyond the Sun, is about 4.24 light-years away. How far is that in kilometers?

4.24 ly × 9.46 × 10¹² km/ly ≈ 4.0 × 10¹³ km

About 40 trillion kilometers. The light you would see from it tonight left in roughly 2022.

1.3

A scale model you could walk

Numbers like 40 trillion kilometers carry little meaning on their own, so shrink everything by a factor of ten billion. At that scale the Sun is about the size of a grapefruit (14 cm across). Earth is the size of a ballpoint-pen tip (about 1 mm) orbiting 15 meters away. Jupiter is a marble about 78 meters out, and Neptune is a peppercorn nearly half a kilometer from the grapefruit.

The striking thing about this model is how empty it is. The planets are specks separated by huge distances. And on the same scale the nearest star, another grapefruit, would be about 4,000 kilometers away: roughly the distance from New Jersey to California.

Think of it this way

Picture the stars of the Milky Way as grapefruits spread across the United States, each one thousands of kilometers from its nearest neighbor. That is why two galaxies can pass through each other with almost no stars colliding: it is like two swarms of grapefruits, spread across two continents, drifting through one another.

Worked example: building the model

In the 1-to-10-billion model, where would Mars go? Mars orbits at about 1.52 AU, and 1 AU is 1.5 × 10¹¹ m.

1.52 × 1.5 × 10¹¹ m ÷ 10¹⁰ ≈ 23 m

About 23 meters from the grapefruit Sun, a little farther than Earth's 15 meters.

1.4

How many stars are there?

On a clear, dark night you can see about 2,000 to 3,000 stars with your unaided eye at any one time. That feels like a lot, but it is a tiny, local sample. The Milky Way alone contains a few hundred billion stars.

How big is "a few hundred billion"? If you counted one star per second, never stopping to sleep or eat, counting just 100 billion stars would take more than 3,000 years. You would have had to start before the founding of Rome.

And the Milky Way is only one galaxy. Deep images from telescopes such as Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope show galaxies in every direction, as thick as grains of sand in a photograph of a beach. Estimates of the number of galaxies in the observable universe run from a few hundred billion to around two trillion. Multiply a typical galaxy's stars by that many galaxies and you get somewhere around 10²² to 10²⁴ stars.

Think of it this way

Scoop up a handful of dry sand and think about how many grains are in it. Now think of every beach and desert on Earth. Rough estimates of all those grains come out around 10¹⁸ to 10¹⁹. The stars in the observable universe outnumber them, likely by a factor of thousands or more.

Pause and think: are we alone?

Astronomers have confirmed thousands of planets around other stars, and it now appears that most stars have planets. With that many stars, is it reasonable to think Earth is the only place with life? What evidence would you need to answer the question? Keep your thoughts in mind: we return to this in Module 6.

1.5

Nothing stands still

Sitting in a chair reading this, you feel motionless. You are actually taking part in at least four motions at once, several of them at astonishing speeds.

Think of it this way

On an airliner cruising at 900 km/h, you can pour a cup of coffee without spilling it. You feel changes in motion, like takeoff or turbulence, but not smooth, steady motion. Earth's motions are about as smooth as they come.

Rotation. Earth's carries us around its axis once a day, west to east. That is why the Sun, Moon, and stars appear to rise in the east and set in the west: we are turning toward them, then away. Your own rotation speed depends on your latitude. In New Jersey it is about 1,270 km/h; at the poles it drops to zero.

Orbit. Earth's (or revolution) around the Sun takes one year and covers nearly a billion kilometers, so we move about 30 kilometers every second. Earth's orbit is nearly circular: our distance from the Sun varies by only about 3% over the year. Keep that number in mind for Section 1.11.

Galactic orbit. The whole solar system circles the center of the Milky Way. One lap takes about 230 million years. The last time the Sun was at its current position in the galaxy, the earliest dinosaurs were just appearing.

Cosmic expansion. On the largest scales, nearly every galaxy is moving away from us, and the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it recedes. This pattern is the signature of an , and running it backward in time leads to the Big Bang, which you will study in Module 13.

Think of it this way

Picture raisins in a loaf of bread dough as it rises. Every raisin moves away from every other raisin, and raisins that start farther apart separate faster, because there is more dough between them to expand. No raisin is at the center; each one sees the same pattern. That is why galaxies receding from us in every direction does not mean we are at the center of the universe.

Pause and predict: is the Milky Way expanding too?

No. Gravity holds galaxies, the solar system, and your body together much more strongly than expansion pulls them apart. (The raisins themselves do not swell as the dough rises.) Expansion shows up in the spaces between galaxy groups, not inside them. That is also why Andromeda can be moving toward us: the two galaxies are close enough for gravity to win, and they may eventually collide and merge, billions of years from now.

1.6

Deep time

The universe is about 13.8 billion years old. Like its distances, its age is too large to picture directly. The is the time version of the scale model: compress all of cosmic history into a single calendar year, with the Big Bang at the first instant of January 1 and the present at the last instant of December 31.

On that calendar, each month represents about 1.15 billion years, each day about 38 million years, and each second about 440 years.

Think of it this way

Or turn time into a walk. Let every 1,000 years be one millimeter, and cosmic history becomes a 13.8-kilometer hike, about the distance from Brookdale's Lincroft campus to the beach at Sea Bright. The solar system appears with about 4.6 km left to go. Dinosaurs vanish 66 meters from the end. All of recorded human history fits in the last 5 millimeters, less than the width of your fingernail.

Worked example: placing an event

The Sun and planets formed about 4.6 billion years ago. What fraction of cosmic history ago is that, and where does it land?

1 − (4.6 ÷ 13.8) = 0.667 of the year has passed 0.667 × 365 days ≈ day 243 → about August 31

Our solar system is a late arrival: it did not form until the last third of the cosmic year.

Several things jump out when you explore the calendar below. Life on Earth appears fairly soon after Earth forms, in September. But complex animals do not appear until mid-December, dinosaurs die out on December 30, and all of recorded human history fits into the final 15 seconds or so of the year.

The sky from Earth

What you can see from where you stand, and why it changes by the hour, the month, and the year.

1.7

The celestial sphere and your local sky

Step outside on a clear night and the sky looks like a dome set over the land, with stars scattered on its inside surface. Ancient observers took that picture literally. We know the stars lie at vastly different distances, but the dome picture remains a convenient way to describe what we see, so astronomers keep it as a model: the .

Think of it this way

A planetarium is the celestial sphere made real: a dome with the stars projected on it and you in the middle. Everything looks equally far away because it is all on the same surface. The real sky fools your eyes the same way. The stars are so distant that your brain cannot tell which ones are nearer, so they all seem painted on one dome.

Your local sky

Wherever you stand, the divides the sky you can see from the half hidden by the ground. The point directly overhead is your . The is an imaginary line running from due north on the horizon, up through the zenith, and down to due south. The Sun crosses it at local noon, and every star reaches its highest point when it crosses it.

To give a position in your local sky, you need two angles. is the angle above the horizon, from 0° to 90°. is the compass direction, measured clockwise from north: 90° is due east, 180° due south. "Altitude 30°, azimuth 225°" means one-third of the way up the sky, toward the southwest.

Constellations

People in every culture have grouped bright stars into patterns. Today astronomers divide the entire sky into 88 official . Strictly speaking, a constellation is a region of the sky with fixed borders, much as a state is a region of the map; the familiar star pattern is just its most recognizable feature. The stars in a pattern usually are not near one another in space. They only happen to lie in the same direction as seen from Earth.

Think of it this way

Look down a street at night and a porch light a block away can appear right next to a streetlight a mile away. They line up from where you stand, not in reality. Stars in a constellation are the same: neighbors in direction, strangers in distance.

1.8

Measuring the sky with angles

Because everything on the celestial sphere looks equally far away, astronomers describe sizes and separations in the sky as angles rather than distances. A full circle around the horizon is 360°; from horizon to zenith is 90°. For finer measurements, each degree divides into 60 , and each arcminute into 60 arcseconds.

An object's is how much of your view it spans. Angular size depends on two things, the object's real size and its distance, which is why a small coin held close can cover the enormous Moon. The Sun and the Moon happen to have almost the same angular size, about half a degree.

Think of it this way

Think of your view as a pizza cut into 360 very thin slices around you. Each slice is one degree. The Moon fills only half of one slice, even though it looks big when it rises. (The rising Moon only seems huge; its angular size near the horizon is the same as when it is high. That is a trick your brain plays, called the Moon illusion.)

Worked example: angular size

For small angles, angular size in degrees ≈ 57.3 × (actual diameter ÷ distance). The Moon is 3,474 km across and 384,400 km away.

57.3 × (3,474 ÷ 384,400) ≈ 0.52°

About half a degree, matching what you measure with your hand in the activity below.

1.9

The turning sky and your latitude

Because Earth rotates, the whole celestial sphere seems to turn around us once a day. It pivots on two points, the , which lie directly above Earth's poles. Halfway between them, above Earth's equator, lies the . Stars rise in the east, arc across the sky, and set in the west, all moving together, like painted dots on a slowly turning bowl.

By luck, a moderately bright star called Polaris sits very near the north celestial pole. Over the course of a night Polaris barely moves while everything else wheels around it. That is why it has guided navigators for centuries: it marks north.

A star's position north or south of the celestial equator is its . It works just like latitude on Earth: the celestial equator is 0°, the north celestial pole is +90°, and the south celestial pole is −90°.

Latitude changes your view

Your controls which part of the celestial sphere you can see and how it moves. The rule is simple and worth memorizing: the altitude of the celestial pole equals your latitude. At the North Pole (90° N) Polaris is straight overhead. At the equator (0°) it sits on the northern horizon. In Lincroft, New Jersey, at about 40° N, it stands 40° above the northern horizon.

Think of it this way

Picture a merry-go-round. Riders near the center barely move; riders at the edge sweep around in big circles. Stars near the celestial pole are the riders near the center: they trace small circles and, if the circles are small enough, never dip below your horizon.

A star whose daily circle stays entirely above your horizon never sets; it is a . From New Jersey, the stars of the Big Dipper are circumpolar, visible on any clear night of the year. Meanwhile, stars close to the south celestial pole never rise for us. To see the Southern Cross, you would need to travel south to about the Florida Keys or beyond.

1.10

The year in the sky

Earth's orbit makes the Sun appear to drift slowly eastward against the background stars, about 1° per day, completing a circle each year. That yearly path is the . The constellations along it form the .

We cannot see the stars behind the Sun in daylight, but the effect shows up at night: the constellations visible at 9 p.m. in winter (Orion, for example) differ from the ones visible at 9 p.m. in summer (Scorpius). At night you are looking out the dark side of Earth, and as Earth moves around its orbit, that side faces a different part of the sky.

Think of it this way

Imagine walking in a slow circle around a campfire in a dark field ringed by trees. The fire's glare hides the trees directly behind it, and you only see the trees on the side away from the fire. As you walk around, the fire seems to move in front of different trees, and the trees you can see change. You are Earth, the fire is the Sun, and the trees are the constellations.

1.11

Why we have seasons

Activity 1.8

Fact or myth? Test your ideas first

About 5 minutes. Decide before you read on; the explanations will make more sense once you have committed to an answer.

Ask a group of people why summer is warmer than winter, and many will say that Earth is closer to the Sun in summer. It is a reasonable guess, and it is wrong. Here are two quick ways to see that it cannot work:

  • The seasons are opposite in the two hemispheres. When it is summer in New Jersey, it is winter in Argentina. Both are the same distance from the Sun.
  • Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, in the middle of the Northern Hemisphere's winter. The difference is only about 3%, too small to matter much.

The real cause: axis tilt

Earth's rotation axis is tipped about 23.4° from perpendicular to its orbit. That is its . The key detail is that the axis keeps pointing the same direction in space (toward Polaris) all year long. So as Earth travels around the Sun, first one hemisphere and then the other leans toward the Sun.

When your hemisphere leans toward the Sun, two things happen together:

  1. The Sun climbs higher at noon. Sunlight arriving at a steep angle is concentrated on a smaller patch of ground.
  2. Days are longer. The Sun spends more hours above the horizon, so the ground has more time to warm and less night to cool.
Think of it this way

Shine a flashlight straight down on a table and you get a small, bright circle. Tilt the flashlight and the same light spreads into a long, dim oval. Summer sunlight is the straight-down flashlight; winter sunlight is the tilted one. Longer days add a second effect: it is like sitting beside a campfire for 15 hours instead of 9.

Both effects increase the total sunlight you receive in a day. Six months later your hemisphere leans away, and both effects reverse.

Solstices and equinoxes

Four points in Earth's orbit mark the seasons. At the June (around June 21) the Northern Hemisphere leans most toward the Sun: longest day, highest noon Sun. At the December solstice (around December 21) it leans most away. Between them come the March and September (around March 20 and September 22), when neither hemisphere leans toward the Sun and day and night are nearly equal everywhere.

You may notice that the hottest weather comes weeks after the June solstice. Oceans and land take time to warm up, just as a pot of water keeps heating for a while after you turn the burner to high.

Earth's axis does not stay fixed forever. Like a spinning top whose handle slowly traces a circle, Earth's axis wobbles, sweeping around once every 26,000 years. This means Polaris is our pole star only temporarily; about 12,000 years from now, the bright star Vega will lie near the pole instead. Precession does not change the amount of tilt, so it does not change the basic pattern of seasons.

1.12

The Moon's phases

The Moon makes no light of its own. At every moment, the Sun lights exactly half of it, the half facing the Sun, just as it lights half of Earth. What changes during the month is how much of that sunlit half faces us. That changing view is the .

Think of it this way

Think of a person standing under a single streetlight, with you walking in a circle around them. The side of their face toward the light is always lit. From some spots you see their whole lit face, from others only a sliver of it, and from the far side almost none, even though the lighting on them never changed. Only your viewpoint did.

The cycle begins at new moon, when the Moon lies roughly between Earth and the Sun. Its lit side faces away from us, and it is up during the day, lost in the Sun's glare. Over the next week, a sliver of the lit side swings into view as a waxing crescent in the evening sky. A week after new moon, the Moon is a quarter of the way around its orbit and we see half of its face lit: first quarter. The lit portion keeps growing (waxing gibbous) until, about two weeks after new, Earth lies between the Sun and the Moon and we see the whole lit face: full moon. Then the sequence runs in reverse (waning gibbous, third quarter, waning crescent) back to new.

A full cycle of phases, the , takes about 29.5 days. The Moon actually completes one orbit relative to the stars a little faster, in about 27.3 days (the ). The difference arises because Earth moves along its own orbit during that time, so the Moon needs about two extra days to return to the same position relative to the Sun.

Common misconception

"Phases are caused by Earth's shadow falling on the Moon." Earth's shadow is involved only during a lunar eclipse, which is rare. Ordinary phases are a matter of viewing angle. At a crescent phase, Earth's shadow is not even pointing toward the Moon.

Phase tells you the time. Because phase depends on the Moon's angle from the Sun, it also sets when the Moon rises. A full moon, opposite the Sun, rises around sunset and stays up all night. A first-quarter moon rises around noon and is highest around sunset. A rule of thumb: each phase step of about a week delays moonrise by about six hours.

The same face, always. The Moon rotates once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to orbit Earth, a pattern called . As a result we always see the same side of the Moon. The side we never see from Earth is often called the "dark side," but that is misleading: it gets just as much sunlight as the near side. It is the far side, not a dark side.

Think of it this way

Walk in a circle around a chair while always facing it. By the time you have gone around once, you have also turned all the way around once, and the chair never saw your back. That is synchronous rotation.

1.13

Eclipses

An eclipse happens when the Sun, Earth, and Moon line up closely enough for one body to cast its shadow on another. Every shadow has two parts: the dark central , where the Sun is completely blocked, and the lighter outer , where it is only partly blocked.

In a , the Moon passes through Earth's shadow. This can only happen at full moon, and everyone on the night side of Earth can see it. During a total lunar eclipse the Moon often turns coppery red, lit only by sunlight bent through Earth's atmosphere: the glow of every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once.

In a , the Moon's shadow falls on Earth. This can only happen at new moon. The Moon's umbra is small where it reaches Earth, so only people inside a narrow path see a total eclipse; a much wider area sees a partial one.

Total solar eclipses are possible at all because of a coincidence: the Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon but also about 400 times farther away, so the two look almost exactly the same size in our sky. When the Moon is near the far point of its orbit, it looks slightly too small to cover the Sun, and we get an , with a ring of sunlight around the Moon.

Why not every month?

If eclipses need only a new or full moon, why isn't there a solar eclipse every month? The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the plane of Earth's orbit. Most months, the new or full moon passes a little above or below the line between the Sun and Earth, and the shadows miss.

Think of it this way

Hold two hula hoops so they cross at a slight angle, like the two orbits. They touch at only two points. The Moon's orbit crosses the plane of Earth's orbit at two points called , and an eclipse can happen only when a new or full moon occurs near one of them.

The Sun lines up with the nodes about twice a year, opening two , each lasting about five weeks. That is why eclipses tend to come in pairs: a solar eclipse and a lunar eclipse about two weeks apart.

Safety note

Never look directly at the Sun, even when it is mostly covered. Use certified eclipse glasses or a pinhole projector. The only safe moment to look without protection is during the brief totality of a total solar eclipse.

1.14

Wandering planets and an ancient puzzle

To the naked eye, the planets Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn look like bright stars. What set them apart for ancient observers is that they move. Over weeks they drift against the background constellations, staying close to the ecliptic. The word planet comes from the Greek for "wanderer."

Usually a planet drifts eastward from night to night. But occasionally it slows, stops, moves westward for a few weeks or months, then turns around and resumes its eastward course. This loop is called , and explaining it was one of the great puzzles of ancient astronomy.

Think of it this way

On the Garden State Parkway, you pass a slower car in the next lane. Against the distant trees, that car seems to slide backward for a moment, even though both of you are driving forward. Earth orbits faster than Mars. When Earth overtakes Mars, Mars appears to drift backward against the distant stars. You will explore this with an interactive model in Module 2.

Why the ancients thought Earth stood still

If Earth really orbits the Sun, then our viewpoint shifts by 300 million kilometers every six months. Nearby stars should then appear to shift slightly against more distant ones. This yearly shift is .

Activity 1.13

Parallax with your thumb

About 3 minutes, anywhere.

Greek astronomers looked for stellar parallax and found nothing. That left two options: either Earth does not move, or the stars are so far away that the shift is too small to see. Most chose the first option, which seemed more sensible than believing in unimaginably distant stars. We now know the second option was correct. Even the nearest star shifts by less than one-thousandth of a degree, far below what the eye can detect. Stellar parallax was first measured in 1838, with a telescope, and it remains a key method for measuring distances to nearby stars.

This episode is a good preview of how science works, the topic of Module 2: a careful argument drawn from the best available evidence can still reach the wrong conclusion, and better measurements settle the question.

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • Our cosmic address runs Earth → solar system → Milky Way → Local Group → supercluster → universe, with each level vastly larger than the last.
  • Scientific notation keeps huge numbers manageable; each power of ten is another factor of ten, and those factors add up fast.
  • A light-year is a distance, about 9.46 trillion km. Because light takes time to travel, looking far away means looking back in time, out to the edge of the observable universe.
  • On a 1-to-10-billion scale the Sun is a grapefruit, Earth a pen tip 15 m away, and the nearest star another grapefruit about 4,000 km away. Space is mostly empty.
  • The Milky Way holds a few hundred billion stars, and the observable universe holds hundreds of billions of galaxies or more.
  • Earth rotates daily, orbits the Sun yearly, and travels with the Sun around the galaxy every 230 million years, while distant galaxies recede as the universe expands.
  • On the cosmic calendar, the solar system forms around August 31 and all of human history fits into the last seconds of December 31.
  • Positions and sizes in the sky are angles. The Sun and Moon each span about half a degree; a fist at arm's length spans about 10°.
  • The altitude of the celestial pole equals your latitude, which sets which stars are circumpolar and which never rise.
  • The Sun's yearly path is the ecliptic, and the night sky changes through the year as Earth orbits.
  • Seasons come from the 23.4° axis tilt, which changes the Sun's noon height and the length of the day, not from changing distance to the Sun.
  • Moon phases come from viewing geometry, and phase tells you when the Moon rises. Eclipses need a new or full moon near a node, which happens during two eclipse seasons each year.
  • Retrograde motion comes from Earth overtaking other planets. The lack of visible stellar parallax misled the Greeks because stars are very far away.

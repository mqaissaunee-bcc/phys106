Star Birth and Stellar Lives

Every star has a life story written by one number: its mass. Stars are born when cold clouds of gas collapse under gravity. Low-mass stars like the Sun live for billions of years, swell into red giants, and gently shed their outer layers. Massive stars live fast, forge heavier and heavier elements, and die in cataclysmic explosions. Along the way, stars make almost every element in your body.

Part A

Star birth

From cold clouds to newborn stars.

10.1

Stellar nurseries

The space between the stars is not empty. It contains the : thin gas, mostly hydrogen and helium, with about 1% of its mass in tiny grains of dust. Most of it is extremely thin, but in places it gathers into , cold (10–30 K) and dense enough for atoms to pair up into molecules such as H₂ and carbon monoxide. Some giant molecular clouds hold enough gas to make a million Suns.

Dust blocks visible light, so star-forming regions look like dark patches against the Milky Way. But infrared light passes through dust, and infrared telescopes such as the James Webb Space Telescope reveal newborn stars inside, like the famous "Pillars of Creation" in the Eagle Nebula.

Think of it this way

Dust in molecular clouds works like fog: headlights disappear in it, but a thermal camera (infrared) sees right through. Astronomers use infrared the same way to peer into stellar nurseries.

10.2

Gravity versus pressure

For a cloud to become a star, its gravity must overwhelm the thermal pressure of its gas. Gravity wins more easily when the cloud is massive, cold (low pressure), and dense (particles close together). The minimum mass a cloud needs to collapse at a given temperature and density is called the , roughly

minimum mass ≈ 2 solar masses × (T ÷ 10 K)^1.5 × (density ÷ 10,000 per cm³)^−0.5
Think of it this way

Picture a crowd trying to squeeze toward a stage while everyone jostles and pushes outward. A small, energetic crowd stays spread out; a huge crowd, or a calm, tired one, packs together. Gravity is the pull toward the stage; temperature is the jostling.

Collapse often needs a trigger, such as a shock wave from a nearby supernova or a collision between clouds that compresses the gas. Once a large cloud starts collapsing, it breaks into many fragments, each forming a star. That is why stars are born in clusters, and why most stars have companions.

10.3

From cloud to protostar

As a cloud fragment collapses, gravitational energy heats its center. At first the heat escapes as infrared light, so collapse continues. Eventually the center becomes dense enough to trap its heat, and a forms: a hot ball of gas that shines by gravitational contraction, not yet by fusion.

The collapsing gas was rotating slightly, and conservation of angular momentum (Module 3) makes it spin faster, flattening into a spinning around the protostar, the same kind of disk that formed our planets (Module 4). Magnetic fields and the spinning disk also launch narrow of gas out along the rotation axis, which plow into surrounding gas and create glowing blobs called Herbig-Haro objects.

Think of it this way

A protostar is like a kettle warming on the stove: it glows with heat for a long time before it starts to boil. Only when the core reaches about 10 million K does hydrogen fusion ignite and the star "switches on."

A Sun-like protostar contracts for about 30 million years before fusion balances gravity and it settles onto the main sequence. More massive stars form much faster, in less than a million years.

10.4

How big and how small can stars be?

The lower limit. Below about 0.08 solar masses (80 times Jupiter’s mass), a contracting object never gets hot enough in its core to sustain hydrogen fusion. Degeneracy pressure (Module 8) halts its collapse first. Such an object is a : a "failed star" that glows dimly in infrared as it slowly cools.

The upper limit. The most massive stars are about 150 solar masses, perhaps a bit more. Above that, a star would shine so fiercely that the pressure of its own light (radiation pressure) would blow off its outer layers.

Within these limits, nature makes far more small stars than big ones. For every star above 10 solar masses, there are hundreds of stars smaller than the Sun. Most stars in the galaxy are red dwarfs, too dim to see without a telescope.

Think of it this way

Star formation is like making popcorn: most kernels become ordinary pieces, a few become huge puffs, and some never pop at all. The unpopped kernels are the brown dwarfs.

Part B

Stellar lives

How stars of different masses live and die.

10.5

Mass decides everything

A star’s mass at birth determines its entire life story. Astronomers sort stars into three groups:

  • Low-mass stars (less than about 2 solar masses), like the Sun: long lives, ending as white dwarfs.
  • Intermediate-mass stars (about 2–8 solar masses): similar but faster, also ending as white dwarfs.
  • High-mass stars (more than about 8 solar masses): short, brilliant lives ending in supernova explosions.

Massive stars also fuse hydrogen differently. Instead of the proton–proton chain, their hotter cores use the , in which carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen nuclei act as catalysts, helping protons combine into helium much faster.

10.6

The life of a low-mass star

Main sequence. For about 10 billion years, the Sun fuses hydrogen into helium in its core, slowly growing brighter as helium builds up.

Red giant. When the core runs out of hydrogen, fusion stops there and the core, now helium, shrinks and heats up. Hydrogen begins fusing in a shell around it, even faster than before. The extra energy pushes the outer layers outward, and the star swells into a , hundreds of times more luminous and up to about 100–200 times its former radius.

Think of it this way

It seems backward that a star grows bigger and brighter when its core runs out of fuel. Think of a campfire: when the center burns out, the fire spreads into a ring of burning wood around the ashes, and the blaze can actually grow. The shell burning around the dead helium core is that ring.

Helium fusion. When the core reaches about 100 million K, helium nuclei begin fusing into carbon, three at a time (the triple-alpha process). In low-mass stars this ignites suddenly in a . The star then settles down for about 100 million years, fusing helium in its core.

Double-shell burning. When core helium runs out, the carbon core shrinks, and helium and hydrogen fuse in shells around it. The star swells into a red giant again, larger than before, and becomes unstable, pulsing and shedding its outer layers in strong winds.

What remains is the core: a about the size of Earth, supported by electron degeneracy pressure, slowly cooling forever.

10.7

The Sun’s future and Earth’s fate

The Sun is about halfway through its 10-billion-year main-sequence life. It is slowly brightening, about 10% every billion years. In roughly a billion years, that extra sunlight will likely trigger a runaway greenhouse effect on Earth (Module 5), boiling away the oceans long before the Sun becomes a red giant.

In about 5 billion years, the Sun will swell into a red giant roughly 200 times its current radius. It will engulf Mercury and Venus; whether it reaches Earth is uncertain, because the Sun will also lose mass, loosening its grip so the planets’ orbits drift outward. Either way, Earth will be a scorched cinder. Finally, the Sun will shed its outer layers as a planetary nebula and settle down as a white dwarf about the size of Earth.

Think of it this way

Think of the Sun’s life as a very long day. Right now it is early afternoon: plenty of daylight left, but the afternoon is gradually getting hotter. The red giant phase is a blazing sunset, and the white dwarf is the long night that follows.

10.8

The life of a high-mass star

High-mass stars live fast. A 25-solar-mass star fuses hydrogen for only about 7 million years. After that, its core is hot enough to keep going where low-mass stars stop: helium fuses into carbon, then carbon into neon, neon into oxygen, oxygen into silicon, and silicon into iron. Each stage is hotter, faster, and shorter than the last, and the star becomes a , with the ashes of each stage fusing in shells around the core.

Think of it this way

By the end, a massive star is built like an onion: layers of hydrogen, helium, carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon, each burning around an inner core of iron. The final silicon-burning stage lasts about one day.

10.9

Why iron is the end of the line

Why does fusion stop at iron? The answer is the : how tightly the protons and neutrons in a nucleus are held together. Fusing light nuclei into heavier ones, up to iron, makes them more tightly bound and releases energy. Iron is the most tightly bound of all. Beyond it, making heavier nuclei absorbs energy; that is why splitting heavy uranium (fission) releases energy in nuclear power plants.

Think of it this way

Think of nuclei as balls rolling in a valley whose lowest point is iron. Light nuclei roll downhill toward iron by fusing; heavy nuclei roll downhill toward iron by splitting. Energy is released whenever a ball rolls downhill. Once a star’s core is iron, it sits at the bottom of the valley: nothing left to release.

10.10

Supernova

Once a massive star’s iron core grows beyond about 1.4 solar masses, electron degeneracy pressure (Module 8) can’t support it. The core collapses in less than a second, from roughly Earth’s size to a ball of neutrons about 20 km across. Electrons and protons combine into neutrons, releasing a flood of neutrinos. The collapse stops abruptly when neutron degeneracy pressure takes over, and the outer layers rebound and are blasted outward in a , which can briefly outshine an entire galaxy. The core is left behind as a neutron star, or, for the most massive stars, a black hole (Module 11).

Think of it this way

The collapse is like a building whose foundation suddenly vanishes: the floors crash down all at once, strike the hard foundation below (the new neutron core), and the rebound, driven by a blast of neutrinos, throws debris outward.

In 1987 a supernova exploded in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy near ours. Detectors in Japan and the United States caught about two dozen neutrinos from it, a few hours before the light arrived, just as the theory predicted. In our own galaxy, the red supergiant Betelgeuse is a future supernova candidate, though it could be anytime in the next 100,000 years.

10.11

We are made of star stuff

The Big Bang made almost nothing but hydrogen and helium (Module 13). Nearly every other element was made by stars, a process called :

  • Carbon, nitrogen, and some oxygen are made in low- and intermediate-mass stars and released in winds and planetary nebulae.
  • Oxygen through iron are made in massive stars and scattered by supernovae. Elements with even atomic numbers (oxygen, neon, magnesium, silicon, sulfur) are especially common, because massive stars build them by adding helium nuclei (2 protons) one after another.
  • Elements heavier than iron are made by neutron capture. Slowly, over thousands of years, in aging giant stars (the s-process); and rapidly, in the extreme conditions of supernovae and neutron star mergers (the r-process). In 2017, telescopes watched two neutron stars merge (Module 11) and saw the spectral signatures of freshly made heavy elements, including gold and platinum.

Each generation of stars enriches the galaxy with heavier elements, which become part of the next generation of stars and planets. As Carl Sagan put it, we are made of star stuff: the carbon in your cells, the oxygen you breathe, the calcium in your bones, and the iron in your blood were all forged in stars that lived and died before the Sun was born.

Think of it this way

The galaxy is like a family recipe passed down and improved over generations: each generation of stars adds new ingredients to the pot before handing it on. The Sun and Earth were cooked from a mix already enriched by many earlier generations.

10.12

Close binary stars and stellar life tracks

Many stars don’t live alone. In a , two stars orbit so tightly that when one swells into a giant, its outer layers can spill onto its companion. This can change both stars’ lives. It explains the "Algol paradox": in the Algol system, the more massive star is still on the main sequence while the less massive star has already become a subgiant, the opposite of what we’d expect for stars born together. The answer is that the subgiant was originally the more massive star; it evolved first, swelled up, and handed much of its mass to its companion.

Think of it this way

Imagine two roommates sharing a pantry. When one starts to overflow their shelf, their things spill onto the other’s. Over time, the one who started with more ends up with less.

Mass transfer onto a white dwarf can also set off nova explosions and a special kind of supernova, which you will study in Module 11.

Wrap-up

Summary and key terms

  • Stars form in cold, dense molecular clouds in the interstellar medium, often hidden by dust but visible in infrared.
  • A cloud collapses only if its mass exceeds the Jeans mass; cold, dense clouds collapse most easily, and they fragment into clusters.
  • A protostar shines by contraction, surrounded by a spinning disk and jets, until fusion ignites near 10 million K.
  • Stars range from about 0.08 to about 150 solar masses; objects below the limit are brown dwarfs. Small stars far outnumber big ones.
  • Low-mass stars: main sequence → red giant (shell burning) → helium flash and helium fusion → double-shell burning → planetary nebula → white dwarf.
  • The Sun is halfway through its 10-billion-year main-sequence life; in about 5 billion years it will become a red giant and then a white dwarf.
  • High-mass stars fuse successively heavier elements in an onion-like structure up to iron, the most tightly bound nucleus.
  • When the iron core collapses, the star explodes as a supernova, leaving a neutron star or black hole.
  • Stars made nearly all elements heavier than helium; elements beyond iron come from neutron capture in giant stars, supernovae, and neutron star mergers.
  • Mass transfer in close binaries changes stars’ lives, as in the Algol paradox.
